# What the Page Cost

Rusk was at the shelf before the first shift with the book open, and the ring came up without being called, the way it had come every morning since the sign with the three hooks stood over the leaves.

Nothing had moved. The crown lamps burned above the wall; the plant stood under them with its body turned down at the trench, and the open flower was still aimed a hand's width past the patch of soil where the four hands had gone in, at the seat where the root line comes up out of the manifold. The sign over the shelf had not changed by a stroke in nine days: the flower, the long stroke, three hooks at the end of it.

Meren wrote the morning's number at the foot of her column on the infirmary wall. It was the same number as the day before, and the six days before that.

"The farm did what the sign asked," Rusk said. He said it to the ring, with his hand flat on the open page. "A name was given at the roots, once, by a hand that remembers. The register has a hole in it for the year. The book has a line under the sign that asks for nothing. That is everything Hesper has done for nine days, and the wall has not moved a leaf for any of it."

"Write down what you want," Meren said, "and stop making it into a speech."

"Then here is what I want." He turned the book so that the last page lay open towards the crew. "Yesterday this ring made a rule at this wall. A name may be given to the flowering only by a hand that remembers the child, said out loud at the roots, once, and never written. It is a good rule. It cost the farm a year of its register and it will hold you all for the rest of your lives. But the book cannot carry it. There is no line under that sign that says a choosing happened here, what was chosen, or when. The seventh name this farm has spent on this plant stands in one head, and this morning that head is seventy-one years old."

"The rule was made in front of the crew," Meren said. "It does not need your pen to stand."

"Everything else this farm knows stands in that pen." He tapped the page. "Every reading, every request, every price. When the trench hands want to know what the plant asked in the spring, they read the book. When Verrin is dead, what does the farm read?"

"The hole in the register," Meren said. "The way it reads the other six."

Verrin was at the rail with the trench crew, and she did not answer him, and Bea sat above them on the rail with the slate against her chest and watched the officer's hand the way she always watched it.

---

He wrote standing, with the book on his forearm, and the ring watched the pen.

> *The name said at the roots on the fifteenth day is entered under this sign by the officer of Hesper, with the other lines of this plant, so that the farm's choosing stands in the book and not in one memory.*
>
> *I will enter it before the count.*
>
> *Rusk.*

Meren read it twice, the way she read a manifest. "You cannot keep that."

"No," Rusk said. "Not without a hand that will say the name to me."

He put the pen down and went along the ring to the trench, and the crew made way for him, and Verrin came up out of the root bed with soil on her forearms and stood on the steps below the shelf.

"I need the name," Rusk said.

"You heard it on the fifteenth day with everyone else." Verrin looked up at him without moving her feet. "It was said once, at the roots, loud enough for the ring. That was the giving."

"I need it written."

"Then you need a different thing than the one this farm voted for." She wiped her hands on her thighs. "I gave you three terms and the ring took them. The holes stay open and the count stands in the book. The child is told, when she is old enough to hold it, that a name was spent here and will never be given to her. And the name is said at the roots, not written down. You were standing right there when I said the third one."

"I wrote the third one."

"You signed it, and now you are asking me to unwrite it."

"I am asking you to say it once more, to me, so that the farm's own record has it." He held the book up. "If you die tonight, the farm has spent a name it cannot say, cannot write, and cannot read. Six holes in a register and a seventh that nobody can open. What has this farm bought for a whole year of its dead?"

"It bought a name," Verrin said. "You want paper. Paper is what this farm has been buying with hands, and you know it better than I do, because it is your hand on the pen."

"Verrin—"

"I will not say it twice." She said it the way she said things in the trench, flat and final. "Not for a page, not for the record, not for your eye. And you may want to hear this part too, officer. That line is not mine, and no hand of mine is under it. The book charges the name at the bottom. You wrote yours."

---

Two days went by.

On the morning of the seventeenth the boy from the water crew took the green journal off the shelf without being asked and held it up at the wall where the child had held it in the spring, open at the last page, both covers flat, so that every leaf in the crown had the officer's line facing it.

The wall did not move. Not the crown, not the low leaves, not the flower that had been looking at the trench for a week.

"Say it," he said.

"It does not read," Meren said. "The gauge in my ring has not moved for eight days. The book has been open on that shelf the whole time, and the plant has not turned one leaf towards it."

"Then the plant took the officer's tooth without reading a word of what he wrote."

"The plant took nothing. A page was not kept and a price was owed, and the price is not the plant's business." She took the book out of his hands and set it back on the shelf, closed. "The count is the farm's law. That is what the child told this ring a week ago and none of you listened to her then either."

Meren went to Rusk at the end of the shift, where he sat on the steps with his hands on his knees.

"Close it," she said. "Write a line over this one. The count is charged against the request that stands under a line, and a new line over the same sign closes the old count — you did it in the spring with the blood, and the ring watched you do it. One sentence, and the count is gone."

"And the sign has no line under it that says any choosing happened."

"The sign never had one. The sign stood over this shelf for nine years with nothing under it at all."

"Nine years ago there was nobody who could write one." Rusk looked at the shelf. "I can. And if I write over it, then in twenty years there is a book here with six holes in the register downstairs, seven names paid out of the farm's dead, and one line in the book saying a choice was made. I would rather wear the price of the page than strike the page out."

"That is a very fine sentence," Meren said. "I will put it in my column next to the numbers, where it belongs."

---

He woke in the dark of the third morning and did the arithmetic before he opened his eyes: teeth, one gone, from the spring; fingers, one joint gone, from the choosing; and then he lay still and passed his hand over his face and found the third thing.

There was no pain. There was no blood on the pillow. The lid lay down over a smooth place, closed the way a gum closes, and the light of the ring, when he stood at the rail, came into his right eye alone, while the left side of the world was simply not there and not dark either — a closed door in a familiar house.

The ring was already at the wall when he came up, because the news had gone up the spine ahead of him.

"Say the arithmetic," Rusk said.

Meren made him sit on the crate and looked at the lid and the socket under it, and there was nothing on the farm to dress.

"The price is taken in the night, and it costs no blood and no pain, and the farm has known that since your tooth," she said. "You are the first officer of Hesper to pay the third one. There is no fourth one written anywhere. Nine years ago the Reader stopped at a tooth and left the post. You have paid the ladder to its top in seven days and the line is still standing."

"Then the count restarts on the same line," Rusk said. "Three days."

"Three days." She stood back and looked at him and did not soften it. "And nobody in this ring, including the child, can tell you what the law takes when the ladder is finished and the count runs again."

The woman from the second shift came up to the crate and stood in front of him the way she had stood in front of the shelf for nine years.

"Yesterday you would not let the ring strike the page out," she said. "Now the seventh name of this farm has cost an eye, and it is still not written anywhere. Tell me what you bought."

"Time," Rusk said. "The ring's rule stands, the name is where it was, and the farm has three more days to find a way to keep a line that says what it chose."

"Three days is what everybody on this farm always has," she said, and went back to her shift.

---

At the end of the day Rusk took the book down into the trench, opened it at the last page, and held it up where the flower was aimed — at the seat where the root line comes up out of the manifold and into the soil.

He held it there for as long as it takes to count to twenty.

The wall did not move. Not one leaf of the crown, not one leaf at the soil.

"It is looking under my hands," he said at last. "It is not looking at the page."

Bea was on the rail above him with her slate, and she said, out loud, in the voice the farm had made a rule about: "It never looked at the page. It has been looking at the seat."

The boy from the water crew came down the steps and stood beside the seat with his hand on the wheel of the bypass that Meren had opened in the spring. He looked at Rusk, and Rusk nodded, and the boy turned the wheel a quarter turn.

The low leaves by the soil turned towards the seat first, all of them together, the way a hand turns. Then the open flower came up off the trench for the first time in six days, a hand's width, and stood with its face on the line where the water comes in. The crown followed it, in one movement, off the lamps it had held all week.

Nobody said anything for a while.

Then Meren went up the steps and stood in front of her column on the infirmary wall with the chalk in her hand. The morning's number was at the foot of it, the same as the day before. She looked at the gauge, and at the man with one eye at the bottom of the steps, and at the page held open in his hand.

She wrote the new number under the old one, and it was a tenth higher.

"Eight days of writing," she said, "and one wheel."

Rusk closed the book and put his hand on the cover and stayed where he was, in the trench, with the flower off the soil and turned at the line, and the sixth line standing open on the page above the name sign of a plant that had never read anything in its life.
