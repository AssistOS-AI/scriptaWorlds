# The One Who Remembers

Rusk called the farm to the root wall before the first shift, and the farm came, because in thirty years no officer had called it to anything but a reading.

The book lay open on the shelf with yesterday's line and his name under it, and nothing standing against it in the count. The crown lamps burned over the leaves. The flower stood open above the shelf with its face down at the trench, and the sign with the three hooks had not changed by a stroke in eight days.

"The law says the flowering is given a name," Rusk said. "The book has four lines in it and not one of them says what a choosing is. I have read that book for thirty years and there is no such line in it, and no such line in me."

The ring waited.

"So the farm will make one. Here, under the sign, this morning, in front of everybody."

"Say the rest," Meren said.

"There is no dead child on Hesper." He said that to the ring and not to her. "Nine years since the last funeral, and that child carries the plant's name already. The law says a dead child. The farm has none."

"The low ring has a few hundred of them," the woman from the second shift said. She stood at the rail with her hands behind her back. "The register keeps every birth and every death on the page of its year. There are children on those pages. They are only not ours any more."

"Then the farm takes one of them," the boy from the water crew said. He held his bandaged hand up while he said it. "The oldest one. The one no hand in this ring remembers. The plant does not hear names — the doctor said so in this ring four days ago. So let it have a word out of a book and let the ring go back to work."

"Before this farm spends anything else," Meren said, "the child reads the whole sign in front of it. That has been the rule of this ring since the first morning, and the officer wrote it into the book himself."

"I wrote it," Rusk said.

"Then nothing goes on that page this morning until she has read it." Meren put the chalk back in her pocket. "Bea. Go down to the stores. No book, and I am not coming."

---

Iven had the crate out in the middle of the low ring before she was down the last of the steps.

"I heard it from here," he said. "Sit, and put the slate where I can see it."

He drew with the chalk on the bare wood. The flower sign; the long stroke under it; and at the end of the stroke, nothing.

"That is the one from nine years ago," Bea said.

"That is the one from nine years ago." He drew beside it: the same flower, the same stroke, and at the end of the stroke a single hook. "And that is the one before it. I read that one too. Eighteen years ago the farm read it and gave its name the same day. Nine years ago it read this one and gave its name the same day. This morning it is standing at the wall with a shape it has had for eight days and no name in it."

Bea looked at the two drawings and then up the spine.

Three hooks. One hook. None.

"Say what changes," Iven said.

"It is the same shape until the end."

"And the end."

She put her finger near the third drawing without touching the wood, the way she read a leaf. "That is the tip of the blood sign. The blood sign is the long stroke with its tip turned back toward the stem. This is that same turn, three times, one after the other."

"What does the turn say?"

"Blood comes back out of the stem," Bea said. "The tip turns back, so the thing goes back where it came from." She stopped there, and then went on slowly, feeling for the edge of it. "Three turns is not three of anything. Nothing on that wall counts. The sign does not ask for a number. The farm counts. The three days are ours."

Iven sat back against the wall of gauze.

"That is everything I know," he said, "and it took me eleven years and a tooth. Now say why you came down here without the doctor."

"To hear what you wrote."

"I thought so." He put the chalk down. "Nine years ago I read the sign I have just drawn for you, and I read it the way the farm reads: the plant wants a name. So I wrote my reading in the book, and under my reading I wrote the other thing."

"What other thing?"

"That she would be kept." He said it flatly, at the crate. "The farm will keep her among its own: in its register, in its speech, in the mouths of the people who knew her. That was my line. That is the line the book took the tooth for."

"The farm gave her to the flower, and then it took her off the page and off the slate and off the walls, room by room, and the men who did that were me and two hands who are dead now. That is the opposite of keeping. So my line did not come true, and on the third morning the tooth was out of my head while I slept."

"But the farm did everything the sign asked. The name was given."

"The name was given." Iven picked the chalk up and did not draw with it. "The book does not care whether a line is a request or a promise. A line is a debt of the hand that wrote it. Mine said the farm would keep her and the farm did not keep her, and I have not written a line since."

"And who chose her?"

"The year she died there was one name on that page." He looked at the drawing of the nine-year sign. "There was no choosing. There was a hand on a page. That is what the farm calls a choosing, and that is what I am coming up the spine to say to it."

---

They carried the crate up between them, and Iven came with it without being asked, which the ring noticed.

Bea read the three drawings at the wall, standing on the crate, with the crew in front of her and the flower overhead. She said the shape first and the words after, the way she had been taught: the flower sign, the long stroke under it, the end of that stroke turned back three times toward its own stem. Then she said the part the farm had not heard before.

"The hooks do not count. The shape does not ask for three of anything. An end turned back three times is a thing that comes back, and the three is the farm's own law, the three days we keep in the book."

"Then the sign is not counting us down," Meren said.

"The sign has never counted anything," Bea said. "We count. And the count is on the writer, not on the plant."

Rusk stood at the shelf with his hand flat on the book and said nothing.

"Then here is what I have," the woman from the second shift said. "You want the oldest name in that register. The one no hand up here remembers, so that the flower gets a word and nobody gets a wound. I have watched this farm buy a page with hands. Do it this morning and the ring learns that lesson a second time: that a name is paper. And the next time somebody wants a page written, he will open a palm over the soil again, and the child will be standing there to watch it."

"Then what," the boy from the water crew said.

"A name is a name while somebody is holding it." She looked at the shelf, not at him. "Spend one that is being held."

Meren turned the chalk over once in her hand.

"Then here is the rule this farm makes this morning," she said. "The flowering is given the name of a dead child of Hesper, and the only hand that may give it is a hand that remembers the child. Said out loud at the roots, once, by that hand. Not by the officer. Not by the child. Not by the book."

"I wrote in the spring that my own voice would answer at the roots," Rusk said. "That line is in the book."

"That line is in the book, and it was paid for with a finger," Meren said, "and on the morning it came due you could not say one word of it in front of this ring. The law let that line go. The farm has not. That is why the voice is not yours."

Rusk looked at the ring, and the ring looked back at him.

"Take the voice," he said at last. "I will write it."

---

They brought the register up the spine between the boy and two hands of the first shift, and set it on the crate, and the ring read it the way the ring read the gauge.

Its first page was older than anyone aboard. Every year had a page, and in six of those years a page was missing: cut out at the binding, with the stub left in the book, a finger wide.

"Six," Meren said, and counted the stubs with her finger, "in six years."

"Six flowerings," Iven said from the edge of the crate. "And each of those holes is a whole year, not a name: the births with it, the deaths with it. That is what this plant has cost this farm, and there is nothing on any wall that says so."

Then the ring read the names the surviving pages carried, the children who had died in years when the flower was not open. The boy read two out; a hand from the second shift stopped in the middle of another, because reading a dead child's name in a ring is not the same as reading it alone.

Nobody in the ring could put a face to any of them.

"Put your finger on it," Verrin said.

She was the oldest hand on Hesper; she worked the trench and had stood at the rail all morning without a word. She came down to the crate and put her hand flat on the open page. "The year a crate came off the rack in the spine and killed the girl who had the bunk above mine. I was seven. Her mother went down on the next manifest and stayed down, and there has been nobody else to hold her since. She is the one on these pages that is still being carried."

The ring was quiet.

"Then the rule can be kept," Meren said.

"With three things." Verrin kept her hand on the page. "The holes stay open, and the number stands in the green journal with the line, so the farm's count of what it spent stands next to the count of its days. When the Reader is old enough to hold it, she is told that a name was spent here and will never be given to her, so that the silence is a thing the farm knows and not a hole in it. And I say it in the trench, over the soil where the hands went in, because that ground is the only thing on this farm that has ever been given anything."

"Write it," Meren said to Rusk. "And write it so that it asks for nothing."

Rusk wrote, standing at the shelf with the book on his forearm, and the ring watched the pen.

> The flowering has been given a name. The name is a child of Hesper who died in a year when this plant was not flowering. It was said once at the roots by the only hand that remembers her. It is not written here and it will not be said again. The farm has spent seven names on this plant, and the register keeps the holes. This line asks for nothing.
>
> Rusk.

---

The ring went down into the trench the way it went to a reading. The crew stood on the steps; Bea sat on the rail with the slate against her chest; Rusk stood at the bottom with the book open on his arm.

Verrin stepped down onto the bare soil where the four hands had gone in. She did not stand on the dark patch; she stood beside it.

She said the name once. It was a word of two sounds, said at the ground, loud enough for the ring.

Nothing in the wall moved. Not a leaf of the crown, not the low leaves, not the flower that had been looking at the trench for five days. The ring stood in the trench and waited past the point where anything could still arrive.

The woman from the second shift stood at the rail through the whole of it. Nine years ago she had not been in the ring.

Rusk signed the line. Meren wrote the morning's number at the foot of her column on the infirmary wall, and it was the same number as the day before.

Iven carried the register back down the spine under his arm, to cut the year's page out of it with the others, and nobody went with him.

Bea stayed on the rail with her head tipped back, looking up the wall where everyone else had stopped looking.

The flower was still open, and its face was not on the dark patch where the four hands had bled. It was a hand's width past it, at the seat where the root line comes up out of the manifold into the soil.

For five days the farm had stood under that flower and said the plant was looking at the blood, and nobody had looked where the flower was looking.
