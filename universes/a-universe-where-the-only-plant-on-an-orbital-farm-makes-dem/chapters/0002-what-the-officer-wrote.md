# What the Officer Wrote

By morning the buds had begun to open and the air gauge in the work ring had lost a tenth of a point overnight.

Meren wrote the number on the infirmary wall, where she kept all her numbers in a column, and then walked the ring to the root wall to look at the plant for herself.

The flower sign was still up, high, six leaves wide, the way Bea had copied it onto her slate the night before. Under it, low down where the roots left the soil and went into the floor grates, the leaves had turned into something that had not been there at dusk.

A stroke going up. At the top of the stroke, two hooks turned toward each other, the way two hands turn when they are holding a bowl between them.

Bea sat under it with the slate on her knees and the chalk in her fist, and she was not drawing.

"Read it," Meren said.

"I do not know that one." Bea did not look away from the leaves. "It came in the night. It is not in the chart and it is not on the wall."

"Say what you think it is."

"I do not think. I read." She said it the way a child says a thing that belongs to her. "Bring me a lamp. It is high up and it is dark, and I cannot see the top of it."

Then the leaves began to move, all of them at once, slow and long, and the crew stopped working to listen to it. The floor carried the trembling better than the air did. Three days of it was still in everybody's arms.

---

Rusk came in with the green journal under his arm and his sleeves rolled to the elbow, out of habit, the way another man wears a watch.

He stood under the leaves and looked at the small shape for a while, and nobody said anything, because after thirty years of writing the book he was the one who could find any sign in it.

"Long stroke with the bend at the end," he said. "That is blood. And the mark above it, the bar across, that is the one the old Reader drew for a request that comes back." He sounded the line out to himself the way he always did before writing. "It asks for blood again."

"It does not have the bar," Bea said. "It has two hooks."

"It has the stroke and a mark above it."

"Those are not hands."

"Then tell me what they are."

"I told you. I do not know that one yet."

Rusk turned to the crew, and his voice was reasonable, and that was the worst of it.

"Three days ago we did a thing that has never been done on this farm," he said. "A child said a word at this wall and the farm moved water and iron for it, and it was right, and I wrote it in the book in her own words and I would do it again. But today the child stands here and says: I do not know that one. And the book has no page for a Reader who does not know. So somebody has to read, or somebody has to pay, and I am not going to stand in front of a shaking plant with an empty page and tell the crew that the rule is working."

"Write nothing," Meren said. "You have written nothing for three days and nobody has lost anything."

"And the air?" He pointed at the gauge over the door, which was a lie and both of them knew it, because the plant's trembling had never once been about the air until now. "It is flowering, Meren. It will want what it wants, and I have a book to write it in, and I would rather write a wrong line than no line."

"You already wrote a wrong line. It cost three arms."

"I wrote what the chart said, and the chart was wrong, and I have not made that mistake twice." He was quiet for a moment. "I have made it once. This is not the same mistake. This is blood again, and the sign says so, and if the sign is wrong then the plant will tell us the way it always tells us, by not stopping."

He opened the journal at the next clean page and wrote the date, and then, in his square officer's hand:

*The plant asks for blood again.*

Under it, on the line below, he wrote his own name. Nobody on Hesper had ever written his name in the green journal. The book was the farm's, not a man's.

Meren saw the name and understood what it meant before he said it.

"You have just written yourself a debt," she said. "If nobody cuts in three days, the count runs out on the man who wrote the line."

"Then it runs out on me."

"Rusk."

"I am sixty years old. I have had a tooth longer than the child has had a name." He closed the book. "Write that in your numbers."

---

Before noon he went down to the low ring.

Iven had read the leaves for eleven years before Bea, and had lost a tooth for reading one of them wrong, and had not read anything since. He worked the stores now. He was stacking boxes of gauze against the wall, and he did it slowly, and he did not look up.

"I want to know what it is like," Rusk said. "The tooth."

The old man set the box down.

"There is no pain. That is the part the children get wrong. You sleep, and in the morning there is a hole." He touched his jaw with two fingers, at the side, where the gap was, and then took the fingers away. "And the hole does not bleed."

"Nothing else?"

"It was never there. That is the whole of it." He picked the box up again. "You know what I did with the rest of that year? I drew the chart. Eleven years of signs and one word next to each of them, so that nobody on this farm would ever have to read a leaf again. You have been writing out of my chart every morning since. So do not come down here asking me what a tooth is like. Ask me what a chart is like."

---

Nobody cut.

The boy from the water crew offered his hand on the second morning, in front of everybody, the way he had offered it in the spring, and Meren stood between him and the shears with the old chart still in the drawer behind her and the promise she had made out loud in front of witnesses.

"The Reader has not spoken," she said. "That is the whole rule. There is no version of it where the arm comes off for a shape nobody has read."

The boy looked at Rusk, who was standing at the wall with his sleeves rolled, and Rusk did not say anything at all, which was answer enough.

The plant kept repeating the small shape. It came every few hours, brighter each time, and each time the hooks at the top of the stroke turned a little further toward each other, and the flower stayed half open like a hand that had forgotten what it was doing. On the second evening the gauge lost another tenth, and Meren wrote that number down too.

On the third morning, Bea asked for a hand lamp.

She did not ask Meren or Rusk. She asked the woman from the second shift, who was closest to the shelf, and she took the lamp and went and stood under the leaves and held it up.

The crown of the root wall was the darkest part of the ring. It always had been. Every leaf up there turned toward the lamp at once — not the whole wall, just the crown, hundreds of them leaning into the little light like people at a door. They followed it when she moved it. When she took it away, they turned back to the dark.

Bea stood there with her arm up and her mouth open, and then she said it, out loud, at the roots, in her own voice, because that was the rule now.

"It is the two bowls over the crown. It wants them filled."

Meren looked up at the crown, and then at the two lamp arms bolted over it with their bowl-shaped shades, which had been dark for two years, since the ballast had been struck off the manifest and had not come back.

"Her reading," she said slowly, "is that the plant wants the lamps."

"Yes."

"Then Rusk has written a line about blood on a morning when the plant was asking for light."

---

Rusk took the news the way he took the numbers on the infirmary wall.

"How much light," he said.

"Two arms. The draw is about the water pumps on the low ring, or the heat on the third shift." Meren had already done it in her head, and she did not like it. "We can take the arms off the work ring. They are the same fittings. We cannot run both."

"Then we run the crown."

"The infirmary lamp is one of those arms."

"Then you will work with a hand lamp for a while, and I will sign the draw." He said it before she had finished being sorry about it, and that was the officer in him, and it was also the first time in three days that anybody on Hesper had decided anything. "The plant is asking for the thing the farm can give. I am not going to be the officer who had it in his hand and wrote blood on the page instead."

It took the crew the whole of the third day. The arms came off the brackets in the work ring and went up the spine on a sling, four people to an arm, and the ring went dark behind them shift by shift. Meren took down the infirmary lamp herself and did not say anything about it. Rusk signed the draw in the log and stood under the crown and watched the cable go up the wall.

The arms came on at dusk.

The leaves did not shake. They leaned, the whole crown of them, into the light, and then the small shape went out of the leaves as if it had been wiped off, and something else came up in its place: the flower, properly open, wide, with the stem bent twice and the hook turned all the way around and the middle of it pale and moving.

The trembling stopped. The gauge began to climb while Meren watched it.

Nobody cut anything, and nobody cheered either. They stood in the new light and looked up at the flower, and the woman from the second shift said, out loud, in the quiet, "That is what it wanted."

---

Rusk woke on the fourth morning with a hole where his left front tooth had been.

He found it with his tongue before he found it with his hand. There was no pain and no blood, exactly as the old man had said, and the gum had closed over the place as if it had been born that way. He sat on the edge of his bunk for a while. Then he got up, and put on his shirt, and went to the root wall, because that was where the farm was.

The crew was already there. Nobody had sent for anybody. Bea was under the leaves with her slate, and Meren was standing where she could see his face.

He did not tell them. He did not have to. He said good morning, and the word came out with a whistle in it, and every adult in the ring heard the whistle, and the ones who had been on Hesper long enough did the arithmetic of the last three days on their own.

The flower was open, and above it, on the leaves, a new shape was coming up slowly, the way a hand comes up out of water: a stem bent twice, which was the flower sign, and under it a long stroke with three hooks, which was not in the chart.

Rusk looked at it and knew what it was, because he had been an officer for thirty years and the farm had done this before.

"It will ask now," he said.

The book was under his arm. He opened it at the clean page, the one after the line about blood, and stood there with the pen in his hand and did not write.

"Whose name?" Bea said.

Nobody answered her. Meren thought of the column of numbers on the infirmary wall and of nineteen years on this farm and of the one funeral in all of them, and she said what the arithmetic said.

"There is no dead child on Hesper to give it. There has not been one in nine years."

Rusk put his hand flat over the open page, the way a man covers a wound.

"There is one name on this farm," he said. "We said it once. Then we stopped saying it, and we have not said it since, and we never will."

Bea waited for one of them to write something.

Neither of them did.
