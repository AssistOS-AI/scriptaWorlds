# The Name He Did Not Say

The shape over the flower finished coming up in the night, and by the first shift it stood as clear as anything the plant had ever said: the stem bent twice, and under it a long stroke with three hooks, one after another.

Bea stood below it with the slate against her chest and did not copy it. She had copied it at dusk, when it was only half out, and there is no sense in drawing a thing twice.

"Read it," Meren said. The ring had stopped working for it, the way it stopped when the plant shook.

"*Give me a name.*"

She said it at the roots, in her own voice, because that was the rule. Then she stayed a moment longer with her head back.

"That is all of it. There are three hooks and I do not know what they count."

Nobody spoke for a while. It was the answer the farm had waited for since the flower opened, and the one answer nobody on Hesper had.

"Then the farm answers," Rusk said. He had carried the green journal up from the office before the shift, which meant he had known how the morning would go.

"With what?" Meren said.

"With a name."

"Whose?"

The ring went quiet. Since the morning the officer had written a line with his own name under it and paid for it with a tooth, the farm had agreed, without a vote and without saying it out loud, that the officer wrote what the child read and nothing else.

Meren said the rest anyway, because she was the one who had made a promise in front of witnesses.

"There is no dead child on this farm," she said. "There has not been one since the last flowering. The sign asks for a name, the farm has no name to give it, and I want everybody standing here to hear that before somebody starts offering pieces of himself again."

"There is a request on that page and nothing under it," Rusk said.

"Then there is a request on that page and nothing under it. The farm can live with a page like that. It lived with three days of shaking and a woman holding a bowl under her own hand for all of them."

"I cannot live with it." He said it in the voice he used for the draw in the log, the voice that made people move. "Nine days ago this book learned to hold the plant's questions. Water: the child reads water, and under it the farm writes water. Light: the child reads light, and under it the farm writes light, and I sign the power and the work ring burns dark for a shift. Now the child reads *give me a name*, and the book stops. It has a page for what the plant wants and no page for what the farm will do about it. That is not a book. That is a bucket."

"You want to say it," Bea said.

"I want the farm to answer. Today that is the same sentence."

The woman from the second shift was standing at the edge of the light, the one who had opened her palm on the first morning and held it open for three days. She had not spoken in front of the crew since the bandage came off.

"I will not be in the ring when you say it," she said.

"That is yours to decide."

"He is right," the boy from the water crew said. Fourteen, twice refused, and he said it too loudly, the way he said everything. "Somebody has to answer the plant. That is what an officer is for. If nobody answers, why does anybody work here?"

"Hear the arithmetic first," Meren said, and she put it in the middle of the ring the way she put a number on the infirmary wall. "You wrote one line in that book and the plant got what it wanted inside three days, and it cost you nothing, because the line came true. You wrote a second line and it did not come true, and it cost you a tooth. Write a third that does not come true, and the law goes down the list. Do you know what is under the tooth?"

"A finger."

"Yes."

"Then I will lose a finger," Rusk said, "and the farm will still be standing here under a flower that wants something none of us will say."

"That is not a line about the plant," Meren said. "That is a line about you."

"It is the only line the farm has."

He wrote it at the shelf, slowly, in the square hand he had used for thirty years.

> *The plant asks for a name. The farm answers with the name it has. I will say it at the roots on the third morning.*
>
> *Rusk.*

Bea came and looked at it upside down, the way she looked at all writing, which told her nothing.

"What did you write?"

"That the farm will answer, and that I will say the name on the third morning."

"You have never said it."

"No."

"Then I will be there," she said, "because I am the Reader and I have to hear it," and she went back to her place under the leaves, and nobody told her not to.

---

For three days the plant did nothing with the written line, because the plant did nothing with writing.

The leaves stood still. The crown leaned into the two bowl shades and stayed leaning. The gauge over the door held the number it had climbed to the evening the lamps came on, and Meren, who had written a number on the infirmary wall every day for nineteen years, wrote the same number down three times and did not like it. Whatever the officer had put in the book, the plant went on being what it had always been: a thing that wanted clean water when the water was bad and light when the light was low, and had never in thirty years answered anything a man had written about it.

Iven came up from the low ring on the second morning.

He had read the leaves for eleven years, had not read one since the year he lost his tooth, and worked the stores now. He came up the spine with his sleeves down and stood under the shape a long time.

"Did you come to read it?" Rusk said.

"No."

"Then why."

"Because nine years ago I drew a chart on that wall so that nobody would ever have to read a leaf again." Iven kept his eyes on the three hooks. "Signs on one side, words on the other. The farm has been reading out of my chart every morning since, and it has been right about as often as a chart can be right. Now you are going to do the same thing at the shelf. You will write a word, then stand at the roots and do the thing the word says, and you think the plant will notice."

"It will not notice," Rusk said. "I know it will not notice. I am not writing for the plant."

"Then say that part out loud to the crew," Iven said, "because the crew is going to watch you fail and think the plant refused you. It did not refuse you. It does not know you wrote." He turned to go back down the spine, and stopped once. "I lost a tooth for a word I put next to a sign. That is the whole story of my life. Do not lose a finger for the same story."

---

That evening Bea asked to hold the book up.

Rusk gave it to her. She carried it to the wall with both hands, open at the page he had written, and held it over her head the way she had held the lamp.

The leaves did nothing. Not the crown, not the low ones by the soil. The plant hung over her with the shape still in it, and the book in her hands was a thing in her hands.

She gave it back, asked the woman from the second shift for the lamp, and held that up instead. Every leaf of the crown turned toward it at once, hundreds of them leaning into the little light like people at a door. They followed it when she moved it along the wall. When she took it away they turned back to the dark.

"Now it can see," Bea said.

Rusk stood with the journal against his chest and looked up at the wall for a while.

"So it has never been listening," he said.

"It is not listening now either," Bea said. "It is looking at the lamp."

Meren went to the gauge and read the number, and wrote nothing, because it had not changed.

"It is not offended," she said. "That is the part I want on the record. The plant is not angry about your line and it is not waiting on it. It is a wall with a flower on it, and the flower is looking at the lamps."

"Then the book was never for it," Rusk said.

"No."

"Then it is for us, and I still wrote it, and I still have to say the name."

---

The crew came to the root wall on the third morning without being sent for, the way they came when the plant shook. Bea was under the leaves with the slate. The woman from the second shift stood by the door with her hands behind her, ready to leave, and did not leave.

Rusk opened the journal on the shelf at his line and put his free hand flat on the page.

"*The plant asks for a name,*" he said. "The farm answers with the name it has."

He got as far as the first sound of it. The name came up in his mouth and stopped there, and he stood with his mouth open long enough for everyone to see that the officer could not do what he had written.

He closed the book.

"Say it," the boy from the water crew said. "You wrote it."

"I wrote it," Rusk said. "I cannot say it."

"Then the farm pays for a line the officer could not carry out."

"Yes."

"Are you reading?" Bea said, from under the leaves.

"No."

"Then what are you doing?"

"Failing," Rusk said. "In front of everybody, which is the only way it is any use."

Up on the wall the plant did what it had done for two days. The crown leaned into the lamp bowls and stayed leaning, the flower kept its face to the light, and the shape with the three hooks stood over the shelf without changing by a stroke. Nobody in that ring would ever know whether the plant had heard a word of the name: it had not moved for the book, and it did not move for this.

Meren looked at the gauge once more, then at the officer's hand flat on the closed book, and wrote nothing down.

---

He woke on the fourth morning and knew before he was properly awake that his left hand was wrong.

The index finger was gone above the middle knuckle. There was no pain and no blood, exactly as the old man had said, and the skin had closed over the joint as if the finger had never been on the hand.

He sat on the edge of his bunk for a while, the way he had sat when it was a tooth. Then he got up and put on his shirt, and found the second button harder than it had ever been, because that was the hand that had always held it.

The crew was at the root wall. Nobody had sent for anybody. Meren had written the morning number on the infirmary wall and come up with the chalk still in her pocket.

Rusk held the hand up where they could see it, because there was no point in making them do the arithmetic out loud.

"Nobody cut anything," the woman from the second shift said.

"Nobody cut anything," Rusk said. "The law does not take it out of the arm that was offered. It takes it out of the man who wrote the line. I have written two lines in this book that did not come true, and I have paid twice, and the plant has not noticed either of them."

"Then stop writing," she said. "Shut the book. Nobody writes, nobody pays."

Meren had thought about that one on the walk up the ring, and she did not like any part of it.

"The law says the requests are written in the green journal," she said. "It is the first thing everybody on this farm knows by heart, before any of the rest. I do not know what happens on a farm where the requests are not written down. Neither do you. Neither does any officer here for nine years. Do you want to find out who the count takes when there is no name at the bottom of the page?"

Nobody answered her.

Rusk turned the book over and looked at the page he had written, at his own name under the line, and then he looked up at the wall, where the flower was still turned to the light and the shape with the three hooks was still standing.

"How long," he said.

"Three days," Meren said. She took the chalk out of her pocket. "The count started again the moment the price was taken. Three days from this morning, and then an eye."

"And after the eye?"

Meren looked at the column of numbers she had kept on the infirmary wall since the year the water went brown, and she said the thing that everybody on the farm had heard since childhood and nobody had ever had to mean.

"There has never been a fourth day."

Above them the crown leaned into the lamplight, and the flower held its face there, and the shape with the three hooks stood over the shelf like a bill the farm had signed and could not pay.
