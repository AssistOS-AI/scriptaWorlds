# What the Wall Stopped Asking

The count restarted on the eighteenth day, so the nineteenth was its first morning, and Rusk had the book open on the shelf before the shift bell.

Meren came up the steps with her chalk still in her hand and read the arithmetic off his face.

"Nineteen, twenty, twenty-one," she said. "The night before the twenty-first. Nothing on the ladder above an eye, and nobody on this farm who can tell you what the law takes when the ladder is finished."

"I am not going to spend two mornings finding out," Rusk said.

The ring came up behind her the way it had come up every morning since the flowering began, and stood along the rail with the shift not started. The crown lamps burned over the wall. The sign with the three hooks stood under them unchanged, and the flower was open above the shelf with its face turned down at the trench.

He turned the book so that the last page lay open towards the crew.

"On the seventeenth day the doctor told me the thing I already knew and would not use. The count is charged against the request that stands under a line, and a new line written over the same sign takes the charge off the old one." He put his hand flat on the page. "I told her then that I would rather wear the price than strike the choosing out of this book. I was wrong about the page. A new line strikes nothing out. Both lines stay, one above the other, and what goes is the count."

"Then what does the new line say," Meren said.

"It says the name."

The ring did not move. Then the woman from the second shift spoke from the back of it, without stepping forward.

"Not by the officer," she said. "That was the rule this ring made on the fifteenth day, with the register open in front of it. Not by the officer, not by the child, not by the book."

"There was a reason for it." Rusk said that to her and not to Meren. "The reason was that a name is a name only while a hand is holding it, and Verrin is holding this one. Verrin is the oldest hand on this farm, and yesterday, in the trench, in front of the ring, she told me she will not say it twice. So this morning the farm has a name in one head, and that head is seventy-one, and two mornings are left before the law takes something out of me that nobody here can name."

The boy from the water crew came down to the first step with his bandaged hand held up, which was his way of asking to be listened to.

"You are going to write a word and call it an answer," he said. "A fortnight of writing in that book. I turned the wheel at the seat and the flower came up off the soil in front of the whole ring, and the doctor's column moved a tenth for the first time since the lamps came on. That is the only thing on this farm that has ever answered anything, and it did not come out of a pen."

"No," Rusk said. "It did not. And I am not writing over your wheel."

"You are writing over the rule," the woman from the second shift said.

"I am writing past a memory." He said it to the rail. "When Verrin is gone there is nothing under that sign that says this farm ever chose anything. The next officer opens the register the way this ring wanted to on the fifteenth day, takes the oldest word on a surviving page, because a word nobody is holding is cheap, and the farm spends another year of its dead on it. You stopped that once. From a grave you cannot stop it again."

"It is not yours to write," she said.

"No," Rusk said. "It is hers. I heard it once, at the ground, and I am going to put it down."

---

Meren took the book off the shelf and read the last page with her finger under the words, the way she read a manifest.

"You heard it once," she said. "Under an open flower, at the roots, in front of the whole ring."

"Once."

"Then write it. And then hand me the book, and I will read the name out loud to these people, so that what goes on the page is heard by everybody it belongs to."

"No."

The ring heard how flat he said it. Meren looked up.

"When this farm spent the last name it spent it out loud," Rusk said, "and then it took the child off the register page, off her slate and off the walls, room by room, until that name stood nowhere at all. This one goes into the book and stays in it. A written name is a name nobody has to say. Your mouth, doctor, is where this farm has always lost them."

Verrin was at the rail with the trench crew and had not looked at the shelf once.

"Paper is quieter than a mouth," she said. "I will give you that. And paper is what this farm reads on the mornings when it wants to know what to do to itself. You are putting her where the orders live."

Rusk opened the book. He wrote standing, with the page on his forearm, and the ring watched the pen.

> *The name said at the roots on the fifteenth day is entered under this sign by the officer of Hesper, from his own hearing, so that the farm's choosing does not stand in one head and nowhere else.*
>
> *This line takes the charge off the sixth line above it and asks for nothing else.*
>
> *Rusk.*

Between the second line and the third he put down the two sounds of the name, the way a name goes into a register, and the ring watched the pen make them, and nobody said them.

He signed it, and closed the book.

"The count is off my sixth line," he said.

"So it is," said Meren.

---

He carried the book along the rail to where Verrin stood.

"Read the entry," he said. "Tell the ring whether I heard it."

"No."

"Verrin. If I heard it wrong, it stands in that book as long as this farm stands."

"Then you had better have heard it right." She wiped her hands on her thighs and looked at the closed book, not at him. "I said it once at the roots, the way this ring told me to say it. I will not say it a second time to check a man's ear, and I will not read a page to find out what I said. What is in there is what this farm will have in nine years, when the hand that reads it never heard her at all."

The woman from the second shift had come the length of the rail and stood looking at the cover.

"Nine years ago this farm took a name off a page," she said. "This morning it has put one on. There is a hand in this ring that can tell you whether you heard it right, officer, and she has just told you no."

---

Nothing happened on the twentieth day.

The book lay open on the shelf at the new line, and the wall stood exactly as it had stood: crown, low leaves, and the sign with the three hooks not changed by a stroke. Meren wrote the morning's number at the foot of her column and it was the same as the day before.

"The book has never moved anything," she said, "and I am not going to stand here and watch it. The plant is drinking or it is not, and that is a thing I can measure."

She sent the boy down to the seat with her orders. He opened the bypass wheel another quarter turn, and the number moved again: a second tenth, the second movement since the flowering began.

"Two movements in my column," Meren said, "and both of them came off that wheel. I have no story to go with them. Put it in your book if you like; I have put it on my wall."

---

The farm came up in the dark on the twenty-first to watch the law take whatever it takes above an eye.

Nothing was taken.

Meren made Rusk sit on the crate and looked at the lid that was already closed and at the hand with the missing joint, and there was nothing on him that was new.

The ring waited past the point where anything could still arrive. The boy from the water crew said out loud what the ring was thinking: the count was off his line, the officer kept his face, and the book had done it — a fortnight of a plant that had never once turned a leaf for a page, and now the law had nothing left to charge.

Then, in the middle of that morning, the flower closed.

Bea saw it first, because she was sitting on the rail with her head back, the way she always sat. The petals that had stood open above the shelf through the whole flowering turned down and came together, and the flower bowed on its stem and lay with its face on the seat where the root line comes up out of the manifold into the soil.

Then the wall let go. The long stroke under the crown untied itself leaf by leaf, and the three hooks came apart last, the way a knot comes apart when the hand holding it opens.

Where the sign had been there were leaves, flat and still, not one of them turned.

"It stopped," Bea said.

"It read it," said the boy from the water crew. "The morning after the officer writes the name, it puts the whole sign away and closes."

"The plant has never read anything." Meren said that to the ring and not to him. "It stood in front of an open book in the spring and did not move, and it stood in front of the same book over that seat a week ago and did not move."

"Then why did it stop this morning," the boy said.

"I do not know," Meren said. "I have a column with two numbers in it, and both of them moved for a wheel."

Bea slid down off the rail and stood under the empty wall with the slate against her chest, and the ring asked her what the sign said now.

"There is no sign," Bea said. "It is not asking any more. It did not answer. It stopped asking."

The ring did not take that up, because it had a quiet wall on its hands for the first time in the book, and every hand on the farm could see that the plant was done, and only the child had said the word *stopped*.

Rusk stood at the shelf with the book closed under his arm and did not say which of them he believed. On the page, under the sign that was no longer on the wall, both lines stood: the sixth, which he had not kept, and the seventh, which had cost him nothing.

The woman from the second shift went along the rail to the steps.

"Nine years of quiet, then," she said. "And then it flowers again and asks again, and the name comes off that page, officer, in your writing, and there will be nobody left in this ring who ever heard her. You have bought this farm nine years of not having to remember."

---

The shift started late, and the crew went about the farm.

Rusk went down into the trench slowly, because with one eye the steps have no depth and he had learned to feel for them. At the bottom he stood at the seat where the wheel is, where the root line comes up into the soil, and looked at the line he had written and at the flower lying over it with its face on the water.

Bea came down after him. She stood at the edge of the soil and looked at the leaves along the root line for a while without saying anything.

"Officer," she said at last. "That one is new."

In the leaves that lie along the pipe, where the root line comes out of the manifold, a shape had come up while nobody was watching: low, small, no bigger than a thumbprint, and turned at the water.

"I do not know that one," Bea said.
