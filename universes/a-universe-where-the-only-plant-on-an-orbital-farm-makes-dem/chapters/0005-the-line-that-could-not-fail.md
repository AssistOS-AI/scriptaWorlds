# The Line That Could Not Fail

Rusk was at the shelf before the first shift, with the book open and the pen wet, and the crown burning over his head the way it had burned for four days.

He had done the arithmetic twice on his bunk. Three lines in thirty years; two of them paid for with a front tooth and the first joint of an index finger. In the morning the count came for an eye, and the page would still be holding a request the farm had answered with patience.

Meren came up the ring with the chalk in her pocket and stopped in front of the shelf.

"Put it down."

"Nothing has gone on this page for two days," Rusk said. "The plant asks for a name. The book says the farm has not read the sign. In the morning the count comes, and the page is still clean."

"That is what reading is. You wait for the whole of it."

"We have waited for the whole of it." He turned the book so she could see the last page, and the empty half under it. "The child read the sign end to end in the lamp light and came back with three hooks and no word. She will come back with the same tomorrow, and the same in nine years. I am the officer of this farm. The reading of the leaves is written by the officer; that is what the post is. It was written that way for thirty years before this month."

"Before this month the officer read *blood* off that wall for three days while the water in the trench ran brown."

"And then the child read *iron in the water*, and the line was opened, and the plant stopped shaking." Rusk put the pen down on the page, not beside it. "I wrote a wrong word once. That is not a reason to hand the book to a seven-year-old and stand at the roots with nothing to say."

"You made the rule with me in this ring," Meren said, "in front of the crew, and you are breaking it in front of the child it was made for."

Bea was at the wall with the slate against her chest. She had been standing there long enough to hear all of it, and she did not say again that her reading had been a half reading. She stood where the Reader stood and watched the officer's hand.

"Read me the sign," Rusk said.

"The flower sign. The long stroke. Three hooks at the end of it."

"And the hooks."

"I do not know what the hooks ask."

"Then I will write the part the whole farm agrees on," Rusk said, and wrote.

> *The plant asks again, as it asked in the spring. The farm knows this asking: blood over the roots, unbandaged, until the plant is still.*
>
> *I give the first.*
>
> *Rusk.*

---

Meren read the line the way she read a manifest, twice, and then she asked the question that was in it.

"Until the plant is still."

"Yes."

"It has not shaken since the night the lamps came on. Four days."

"I know what I wrote."

"You wrote a line this farm has already kept." Her voice did not rise; she had found the number, and the number was the whole point. "There is nothing in that line for the plant to refuse."

"There is a line in that book," Rusk said. "Four days ago I stood at the roots and could not say the word under the last one. This morning the book has a page under the sign that the farm can pay."

He took the short knife off the hook beside the shelf and walked to the root bed, where the soil lies bare in the trench.

"Do not," Meren said.

"I am not bleeding for a word," Rusk said. "I am bleeding for a page."

He knelt and opened the palm of his left hand over the dirt — the hand with the gap above the middle joint of the index finger — and let the blood run into the soil the way the crew had let it run in the spring, and stayed kneeling with his fingers open.

Nothing happened for as long as it takes to count to ten.

Then the wall turned.

It came down and across in one movement, the way water comes down a sluice: every leaf of the crown swung toward the kneeling man, the low leaves by the soil turned after them, and the open flower, which had held its face into the two bowl shades since the night the lamps came on, tipped over and followed them down, until the whole plant was looking at the wet ground under his hand.

Nobody in the ring made a sound. It was the first time the farm had seen the plant turn for anything a man did with his own body.

"The plant is drinking," the boy from the water crew said. Then he was over the rail with his own knife out, and after him two hands from the first shift, and then a third, and Meren was on the steps with her roll of gauze and no chance of getting there first. Four hands over the soil before the sun came round to the ring.

She bandaged them one after another, standing, without waiting for anything.

"Hold them out," she said. "Under the old rule you would hold them open until it stopped shaking. It stopped shaking four days ago. There is nothing to wait for. You are buying a scar."

"It turned," the boy said. "You all saw it turn."

"I saw it turn," Meren said. "I have seen it turn for a hand lamp."

Bea had come down to the rail and was watching the flower with her head tipped back. "Every time I moved the lamp, the whole crown went with it," she said. "Along the wall. Like that."

"The child is right," Meren said.

"She is right," Rusk said. He was still on his knees while the gauze closed over his palm. "I know she is right. It will not matter."

"Say why it will not matter, because I want that on the record."

"Because the plant does not read," Rusk said. "It never read anything. It turns to what is warm and wet, and it turned to a cut hand for the same reason it turned to a lamp. And the book is the only thing on this farm that has ever told forty-one people what to do. I am not leaving it empty while the count goes for my eye, and I am not the first officer to write down what he believed."

Up on the wall the flower stayed where it had turned, face down toward the soil, and did not come back to the lamps.

---

The woman from the second shift came up while they were clearing the trench. She stood at the rail and looked at the four bandaged hands, and then at the stain in the dirt, and she did not take her knife out.

"In the spring I held mine open for three days," she said, "and the water came through the line brown the whole time, and I got nothing for it. This morning the farm pays for a page."

"Go back to your shift," Meren said.

"Nine years ago the book had a line like that one." The woman said it to the shelf, not to either of them. "And the farm paid it with a name. This year it is paying with hands, which is cheaper. I would like to know what any of it is buying."

She went back up the spine. Iven had come up from the stores at the end of the shift, and he stood at the edge of the light with his sleeves down and read the new line twice, and then looked at the child a long while, and went back down without saying anything at all.

---

Rusk woke on the third morning and did the counting before he opened his eyes: teeth, one gone; fingers, one joint gone; eyes, both there, and the light over the bunk ordinary.

He sat on the edge of the bunk with his hands on his knees. Then he dressed, which took both hands and three tries at the second button, and went up the ring, where the crew had already gathered at the wall without being sent for.

He held the bandaged hand up where they could see it. Then he turned his face to them and let them see that it was whole.

Meren had the book on the shelf and had read the line again.

"Say the arithmetic," Rusk said.

"There is no unmet request on this page," she said. "The count was running on the name. The book now carries that sign with a line under it, and the farm kept the line the same morning it was written, so the count has nothing to charge. This is the first morning in nine years that a price was due on this farm and was not taken."

"The line could not fail," Rusk said. "Say that part too."

"The line could not fail," Meren said. "You promised blood until the plant is still, and the plant has been still since the lamps came on, so the line was finished the moment the pen left the page. You wrote a page that can always be paid."

"I know."

"Did you know when you wrote it?"

"I knew when I cut." He looked at the bandage. "The cut was not for the plant. It was for the crew, so that the page had a hand behind it and not only a pen."

"And the sign?"

"Unchanged," Meren said. "The plant is not reading the book. Whatever the three hooks ask, it asked it before this morning and it is asking it now."

She took the chalk out of her pocket and went to the infirmary wall, and wrote the morning's number at the bottom of the column. It was the same number as the day before, and the day before that.

"Then here is what I will put on my wall instead of a line in yours," she said. "When the gauge moves, we find out what the farm answered. Not before."

---

The crown lamps burned all that day while the work ring stayed dark, because the margin had gone four days ago to a light the plant had wanted. The sign stood over the shelf without changing by a stroke: the flower sign, the long stroke, three hooks one after another.

But the open flower did not turn back to the shades. It held its face down toward the trench where the blood had gone into the soil, and it stayed like that through the shift, and through the night, and into the next morning.
