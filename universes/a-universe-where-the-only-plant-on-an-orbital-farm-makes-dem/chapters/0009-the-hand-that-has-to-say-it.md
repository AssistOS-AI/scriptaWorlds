# The Hand That Has to Say It

The twenty-second morning came up with nothing standing over the shelf.

The crown lamps burned above the root wall and the leaves lay flat under them, the way they had lain since the sign came apart. The flower lay closed on the seat where the root line comes up out of the manifold, its face down on the water. There was no shape to read and no count running in the book, and the ring came up to the wall anyway, as it had every morning for three weeks.

Meren was at the gauge before anyone else.

"The plant is drinking," she said. "That is the whole of what it ever asked us for. The boy goes down to the seat before the first shift and reads the wheel, and I write what the line takes. Any hand that wants to know what this farm owes that thing can read my wall."

"One quarter turn," the boy from the water crew said from the steps, holding his bandaged hand up. "It came off the trench in front of the whole ring."

"Two," Meren said. "And two tenths. Neither of them came out of that book."

Rusk stood at the shelf with the green journal open at the last page. He had nothing to write under it and he had come up anyway.

Bea was on the rail with her head tipped back, looking at the empty wall. She had looked at it every morning since the flower closed, and the ring had stopped asking her what she saw, because there was nothing to see.

"Reader," he said. "The small one. In the leaves along the pipe at the foot of the trench."

"It is still there." Bea did not take her head back. "It has not grown."

"What does it ask."

She was quiet long enough that the crew began to shift. Then she slid down off the rail and stood under the wall and said it the way she said a reading, in her own voice, with the words after the shape.

"It is not turned at me. It is not turned at the shelf and it is not turned at the ring. Every morning it is turned at the water, the way the flower was turned at the seat." She put her hand flat on the rail. "It is not asking us for anything. It is looking at the line."

Meren nodded once, at the gauge.

"Then the farm's answer is the wheel and the number," she said. "Two hands on the line every shift. Water that has not been through a hole in a valve seat. That is the whole of our religion."

---

"The sign will come back in nine years," Rusk said, "and when it does it will ask for a name, and this morning I want the farm to do the thing it has never done."

He turned the book so that both lines stood open towards the rail.

"Nine years ago there was one name on the year's page and a hand wrote it. This year there was one hand in the ring that could give one, and it gave it, and there was no choosing in that either. So I ask the ring: in nine years, from where does the name come, and who says it out loud."

"From whoever died," the boy said.

"Say the rest of that," Meren said.

"From whoever died in the nine years. There will be somebody. There always is."

"Then we are choosing a child who is not dead yet," the woman from the second shift said from the rail. "This ring is going to stand here and decide whose funeral it needs."

"I did not say it well," the boy said.

"You said it exactly," said Verrin.

She came up the steps from the trench with soil to her elbows, and the ring made room for her the way it always did.

---

They brought the register up the spine, because the ring asked for it, and Iven came with it under his arm, which meant he had been waiting with it since before the shift bell.

He set it on the crate and opened it where the stubs are.

"Seven," Meren said, and counted them with her finger, the way she had counted them on the fifteenth day.

"Seven names and seven whole years," Iven said. "Read them out and you will see why the officer is standing there with a pen in his hand."

So the ring read the pages the register still had. The boy read two names out and stopped, because they were children from a year nobody at the wall had been born in; a hand of the second shift read three more and stopped in the middle of the fourth, because a name read aloud in a ring is not a name read alone.

Not one hand at the wall could put a face to any of them.

"That is the pool," Verrin said, when the reading stopped. "Hundreds of names on those pages, and not one face behind them. Now ask your question again, officer. Who is going to say it out loud?"

"I can teach the reading," Iven said. "I have taught it once. A mouth can be trained onto a page."

"That is not reading." Bea said it from the rail without turning round. "That is saying."

The ring went quiet, and the woman from the second shift took one step forward, which on that farm was a speech.

"There are two children in this ring that anybody could still see if they closed their eyes," she said. "The girl who had the bunk above Verrin's, and mine. Both of them are spent. Mine carries nothing now and may still not be said on this farm, and the other one the plant keeps. So when the next sign comes up and asks, every mouth here will be holding a name it is forbidden to say, and the register will be holding a few hundred names with no face on them at all." She looked at the shelf and not at Rusk. "That is the choosing you want written down. The farm has to choose between a page and a mouth, and this morning it has neither."

---

Two answers stood up in the ring and neither of them fell down.

"A page is a page," the boy from the water crew said, standing on the first step with his hand up. "The plant does not hear a word, the doctor has said so twice, and the man with the pen showed us what a line costs when it asks for nothing: the count came off his head and nothing was taken. So let him write the choosing. The farm gives the next flowering the oldest name in the register, and no child on this farm is ever picked for a funeral again."

"That is the cheapest thing this ring has ever been offered," Verrin said. "It was offered once before. I was standing where you are standing when the farm took a page, and the price of that page walked around this ring for a tooth and a finger and an eye, and it was not the plant that did the taking."

"What did the page cost us, then," the boy said. "Say it in numbers."

"The officer's face," Meren said, with the chalk still in her hand. "And a name in that book that no living hand can check, and a child on the rail who walks past that line every morning of her life. You want the next choosing written the same way."

"I want nobody to die for it."

"So do we," said Verrin. "That is not the choice."

The second answer was Meren's, and the ring knew which way it would go.

"A name is a name while a hand is holding it," Meren said. "The woman from the second shift said it on the fifteenth day, the ring took it, and the officer's pen then did what it liked with it, so I will say the rest of it now. If this farm wants to have a name when the sign comes back, it has to have hands that hold one — hands that knew the child alive, and not hands that read a page and say the sounds. That is all a keeping is. The farm's whole problem is that it has spent both of the children it was still holding, and it has no third."

---

Rusk put the book down on the shelf and left it there, closed, and did not touch the pen again that morning.

"Then say the rule," he said. "I will write nothing. What the ring makes this morning stands in no line of that book, if that is what the ring wants."

"It is what the ring wants," Meren said. "A rule with no line has no count on it, and that cuts both ways, officer, and we have all seen that too."

"Then here is what the ring made on the twenty-second day," Verrin said, and she said it slowly, with the register open beside her. "The flowering is given the name of a dead child of Hesper. The choosing is done here, at this wall, in front of the ring, and never in a room. A hand may offer a child it knew alive. No hand is ever asked for one, because a name said to order is paper. Any hand in the ring may refuse an offering, and the hand that offers it is the hand that says the name at the roots, once, out loud, the way I said mine."

"And the day the name is given," the woman from the second shift said. "What does the farm keep of the child afterwards? Nine years of quiet, in my case, and a hole in a register."

"That is the second thing," Meren said, "and it is the doctor's business. The farm will say its dead out loud. One day a year, at this wall, at the shift bell, the ring says the names of the children this farm has lost — said, not read, from mouths and not off a page. The day the flowering began is the day for it. Whoever holds a name says it, and the child who reads the leaves is to know the farm's dead the way she knows the wall."

Nobody spoke for a moment. Then the boy from the water crew said: "Say the first one."

Meren looked at the register on the crate, and at the seven stubs, and at the woman from the second shift, and at Verrin.

"The first one is this morning," she said. "There is nothing to say. Every child this farm could still name is already the plant's, and the names on those pages belong to nobody here. The keeping stands empty this year, and that is what nine years without a funeral bought us."

Meren wrote the morning's number at the foot of her column, the same as the day before, and the shift started.

---

Verrin took Bea down into the trench at the end of it, under the closed flower, and stood her on the steps where the soil is bare.

"You have been told what the ring voted on the fifteenth day," she said. "I am doing my part of it now, and then it is done. A name was spent here. It was said once, over this ground, by me. It is in no page of that register and no page of that book in any words you can read, and it will never be given to you, and that is not a thing the ring does to you. It is a thing the ring is keeping you out of."

"Will you still have her in nine years," Bea said.

"No."

"Then the farm has to put her somewhere that lasts longer than a mouth."

Bea was quiet, and then she said it in the flat way she read a sign, with the words after the shape.

"Then say them to me. All of the ones you have got. I will hold what you give me, and I will not ask for the one the flower is holding." She looked up. "The keeping is mine. I read the leaves. Nobody else in this ring is still going to be here when the sign comes back."

Verrin stood with soil on her forearms and did not answer her for a while.

"You are seven," she said at last.

"The other Reader was eleven years in the post," Bea said. "And he is the one who told me that the farm writes down what it wants to keep."

---

The woman from the second shift was at the rail when the ring came up for the second shift, and she had waited for the shelf to be empty.

"Officer," she said. "The day the ring made this morning. The keeping."

"It stands," Rusk said. "It is in no line, but it stands."

"Put my daughter in it."

He looked at her with the one eye he had left.

"You know what the law says."

"I have kept it nine years without being asked." She said it evenly, with her hands behind her back. "The farm may not mention her again, because the flower would be offended. Nobody here has ever tested whether that flower hears a mouth at all — you wrote a name into a book in front of me and it read nothing, and my daughter's name has been off every wall for nine years while the plant carried it." She nodded at the flat wall. "So there is my request. It is a request, not a line, and nothing is charged to anybody for it. Say her name at the wall once a year, with the others, and let the ring hear it."

Rusk stood at the shelf with the closed book under his arm and did not answer her, because the ring was coming up behind her and it was her shift, and above the shelf the wall lay flat and unturned over a book that had never moved one leaf of it.
