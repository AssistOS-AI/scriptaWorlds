# The Shape Without a Word

The ring did what it always did with a price: it went back to work. The second shift had the wall, the gauge had to be read, the stores had to come up from the low ring. Rusk stayed at the shelf, because there was nowhere for an officer to go on the morning his own hand had been paid for.

He had read the page twice already with his right hand. The left lay open on the book with the gap above the middle knuckle, and when he closed it, it was the left that did it, badly.

Meren came up the ring with the chalk in her pocket and stopped where Bea stood under the leaves with the slate against her chest.

"Come down to the stores," she said. "Bring the slate."

"What is in the stores?"

"The old Reader." Meren took the folded chart out of her pocket and put it in the child's hands. It was a quarter of its size and thirty years old. "Three days from this morning there is an eye. We have already spent a tooth and a finger learning that a chart is not a sign. I am not spending an eye on it as well."

---

Iven was stacking boxes of gauze against the wall of the low ring, and he did not stop stacking when they came down the spine.

"Teach her," Meren said.

"No."

"She is seven years old and the farm pays for her readings with another man's body."

"I drew the chart so that nobody on this farm would ever have to read a leaf again." He set the box down and looked at her, and then at the child, who was holding his chart the way you hold a thing that has been folded for a long time. "You are asking me to unmake the only useful thing I have done."

"I am asking you to teach her to read the shape and not the word you wrote next to it. There will be another flowering. She has to be ready before it comes."

"Nine years from now," Iven said.

"Two mornings from now," Meren said. "That is when the farm finds out whether it can read."

Iven was quiet for a while. Then he took a crate and pulled it out into the middle of the ring, and put the chart on it, and began to fold the words under.

"One lesson," he said. "And it is not the lesson you came down here for."

---

He worked along the chart and turned every word under its own sign, the way a man folds a hem, so that when he was done there was nothing on the paper but shapes.

"You know this one," he said.

Bea looked at the long stroke with the bend at the end. "That is two signs. Blood if the tip turns back toward the stem. Iron in the water if it has the dash across."

He put his thumb over the tip.

"Now."

"I cannot see it."

"Say the word anyway. The farm needs a word. The farm is standing at the shelf right now, waiting for a word."

"I do not know," Bea said.

Iven took his thumb away. He looked at her the way adults on Hesper looked at a gauge that had just been proved right about something they did not want to know.

"That is the first thing I ever learned," he said, "and it cost me a tooth. Now the second thing."

He pushed the crate under the next sign: the dash over the stem, with the end of the stem forked. The chart said *water*.

"That is not water," Bea said.

"The chart says water. I wrote water. Say the word."

"I do not have the right word. But that one is wrong."

"How do you know, if you do not have the right one?"

"Because of the fork." She said it slowly, feeling her way along it out loud. "Water comes out of the line and it goes one way. This one goes two ways, and nothing in this ring goes two ways except the air."

Iven stood over the crate for a moment and did not correct her.

"Two things, then," he said. "A shape can tell you no. And if you cannot see the end of it, you say so, and you say it in front of adults who want to hear something else. That is everything I know, and it took eleven years and a tooth."

Meren had been standing at the end of the crate with her arms folded, and she had the chalk out of her pocket and did not write anything down, which only happened when a number was already fixed in her.

"Show her the last one," she said.

"No."

"Show her the last one, Iven."

Iven picked the chalk out of her hand, and on the bare wood of the crate he drew the shape that had been standing over the shelf for four days: the stem bent twice, and under it the long stroke, and at the end of the stroke three hooks, one after another.

"How many?" he said.

Bea looked at it a long time. The low ring was quiet, and the vents moved the air the way they always did, and nothing shook.

"It is one line," she said at last, "and the end of it is bent three times."

"How many does it count?"

"I do not think it counts."

"Why not?"

"Nothing up there counts anything." She was still looking at the crate. "When the plant wanted the water clean it did not ask for three waters. It asked, and then it asked again."

Iven did not answer that. He took the chalk back and did not draw anything else with it, which was an answer of a kind.

"You never drew this one," Meren said.

"No."

"Why not?"

"Because I drew the chart in the year the farm read a sign of mine and paid for it, and I did not draw the ones I was not sure of." He put the chalk in his pocket. "There are four signs on that wall that are not on my chart. That is one of them."

---

Meren let him stack two more boxes, and then she asked it.

"Whose name did the farm give it, nine years ago?"

Iven kept his hands on the gauze. "You were the doctor. You were at the funeral."

"I was at the funeral. I was not at the choosing."

"There was no choosing." He said it flatly, in the voice of a man reading a manifest. "There was a sign, and a book, and a Reader who wrote in it."

"The officer writes in it."

"The officer writes in it now." He looked past her, at the chart lying folded open on the crate. "Nine years ago the Reader wrote his own reading, and the book took the price out of the man whose line it was."

"Then it took yours."

"It took my tooth."

Meren stood with that for a moment. It was the first new number she had been given in nine years, and she turned it over the way she turned numbers over.

"And the name?"

"Here is the part that belongs to you," Iven said. "The page is gone out of the register in this ring. Her slate went down the spine with the rest of the stores. Her name is not written on any wall of this farm and it is not said in any ring of it, and I did the taking, one room at a time, because the flower must not hear it. That is what was done."

"That is what was done," Meren said. "I asked how it was chosen."

"I am not answering that."

"Two mornings, Iven. In two mornings the count takes an eye out of the officer because the farm cannot answer a request for a name, and you are standing in your stores folding gauze."

"I am not answering that." Then he said the rest to the child, not to the doctor, and quietly. "I will not put it in your mouth."

Bea had the slate under her arm. "I am the Reader," she said.

"That," Iven said, "is what I am afraid of."

A box moved behind them. The woman from the second shift was standing in the doorway of the stores with her hands behind her back, the way she had stood in the ring on the morning the officer wrote a line about blood. She had opened her palm in front of the crew in the spring and held it open for three days, and no one had ever asked her why she was so quick with a knife.

"He will not answer it because of me," she said. "So I will answer the part that is mine and he can keep the rest."

Meren turned.

"Say it, then," the woman said, "and do not make the child say it back to you. The name the plant carries is my daughter's."

She said the word that was not the name. Nobody in that ring was going to make her do more than that, and Iven, who had taken the name off a page and a slate and a wall, stood with his hands full of gauze and did not look at her at all.

"Nine years," Meren said.

"Nine years," the woman said. "I do not stand under the flower and I do not say it and I do not leave. That is all I have done with it." She looked at the child for a moment. "She was seven," she said, and went back up the spine.

---

On the second morning Meren did the other half of the lesson, at the wall, where the farm could hear it.

She stood over the child the way an officer stands over a gauge and made her read the sign four times: the flower sign, the long stroke, the three hooks. The crew stopped working for it, because the crew stopped working for everything that was about the plant.

Bea read it. Then Meren said the thing that was not a reading.

"Now say the part you cannot read."

"The end of it. I do not know what the hooks ask."

"Say it louder."

"I do not know what the hooks ask."

"Again."

"I do not know what the hooks ask."

Afterwards Bea stood with the slate against her chest, and Meren wrote nothing in the air and nothing on paper.

"How does it feel?" Meren said.

"Like he is going to be angry with me."

"He is going to be missing an eye," Meren said. "That is different. Say it once more and then you may go and eat."

Rusk came to the wall while the child was finishing, with the book under his arm and his bad hand held against his chest the way a man holds a hand he does not trust.

"What do we write this morning?" he said.

"Nothing," Meren said.

"Nothing is a word I can write."

"Then write that the farm has not read the sign yet, and write your name under it, if you want the arithmetic to stay honest."

Rusk looked up at the wall. The crown leaned into the two bowl shades, the flower held its face to the light, and the shape with the three hooks stood over the shelf exactly as it had stood on the morning he lost the finger.

"Four days ago I wrote in that book that the farm would answer with the name it has," he said. "I am not taking the line back. It is mine, and it is in the book."

"Nobody is taking it back," Meren said. "You will pay for it in two mornings either way. What we do with the two mornings is read the whole sign in the light and put nothing on that page until the child has read all of it."

"Read it, then," Rusk said, and he stood there with the book under his arm and waited, the way the whole farm had been waiting since the flower opened.

---

Bea read it that evening with the hand lamp, and it went the way Iven had taught her, shape first and word after. The flower sign was the flower. The long stroke was the long stroke. The three hooks at the end of it were three hooks at the end of it, and there was nothing anywhere on Hesper that said more than that.

Not one leaf moved. The crown held its face into the lamp and did not care what she said, because it had never cared what anybody said.

She gave the lamp back to the woman from the second shift and went down the spine with the slate, alone, without being sent for, and Iven was still in the stores because the stores never closed.

"Draw it," she said.

"Draw what."

"The one from nine years ago."

He took the chalk back out of his own pocket and knelt down, and drew on the bare wood of the crate: the stem bent twice, the long stroke under it, and then the stroke ended, plain, with nothing at the end of it.

Bea crouched down and looked at the wood for a long time.

"There is nothing at the end," she said.

Iven put the chalk down on the crate beside the drawing.

"There was nothing at the end," he said. Then he wiped his hands on his trousers and stood up. "There are more of those. Come down when you want them drawn, and come without the doctor and without the book."

Above them, up the spine, the crown leaned into the light and the flower stood open over the shelf, and the count on the officer's third line had two mornings left in it.
