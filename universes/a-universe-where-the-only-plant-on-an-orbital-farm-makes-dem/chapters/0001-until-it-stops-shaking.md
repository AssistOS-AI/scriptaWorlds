# Until It Stops Shaking

Three arms lay in Meren's infirmary, none of them bandaged, because the plant had not stopped shaking.

The first belonged to the woman from the second shift, who had opened her palm over the roots on the first morning and had held it open since, over a bowl, because the ritual asked for that too. The second belonged to the boy from the water crew, fourteen years old, who had cut himself on the second night without being told, to be useful, and had been praised for it in front of everybody. The third was Meren's own work: on the officer's order, on the second morning, she had opened a hand with her own shears and had regretted it before the blood reached the soil.

All three had bled into the roots. The leaves had gone on trembling.

The trembling was the only answer the plant ever gave, and it carried the whole law of Hesper inside it: a request was met when the shaking stopped. For three days it had not stopped.

Meren tied her hair back and stood where the light was worst, so that she would have to be looked at.

"Nobody cuts today," she said.

She said it to the arms, to the shelf, and to the shears she had not picked up. Then Rusk came in with the green journal under his arm.

He was sixty and had the thinnest blood on the farm. His sleeves were rolled to the elbow, old scars over new ones, and he held his forearms out the way a man holds out a document.

"The third day," he said. "You know what the third day costs."

"I know what your arms cost. If I open you again you will go down at the roots and I will carry you back through the ring in front of everyone."

"I have carried worse."

"You have carried worse *for the plant*. That is the whole of your argument, and it has never once been medicine."

He put the journal on her table and opened it at the last page. Three lines, in his handwriting, one for each morning: *The plant asks for blood.* He wrote them himself, every time, from the chart.

"Say the reading is wrong," he said quietly. "Say it in front of the crew. Then you take the tooth off the woman and put it in your own mouth."

Meren looked at the woman from the second shift, who was not asleep, and who was listening with her eyes closed.

"Three days," Meren said. "Three cuts. Has it ever stopped shaking for longer than it takes to put the shears down?"

"Twice, on the first day. A few minutes." Rusk said it as though it were good news.

"A few minutes is not an answer. That is a bruise. We gave it blood three times and it shook through all three, and tonight the count runs out and somebody pays."

"That is why the third day asks for more."

"And on the fourth day?"

"There has never been a fourth day."

Meren had heard the story of Rusk's ninth year the way everyone on Hesper heard it. He had told the other children about a forest — a thing he had never seen, described to him from a picture in a supply crate — and he had spent a year working the roots in silence for it, with a cloth over his mouth so that the words could not come out. Thirty years and one promotion later he still did not say the word. He said *the place in the picture*. The law had made him careful, and careful had made him officer, and there was no way to argue with a man like that except with the plant itself.

---

She found Bea at the root wall with her slate on her knees.

The wall filled the end of the ring: one wall of the plant, taller than the loading doors, with the roots coming out of the soil and down into the floor grates like something reaching for water. Above them the leaves were in slow motion, all of them at once, the way they had been for three days.

Bea was seven. She had held the Reader's post for eleven months, since the child before her had grown too old for it, and she could not read a written word to save her life. Children read the shapes better than adults; that was in the law, and it was also the plain truth of the thing, because an adult looked at a leaf and saw the letters he had been taught, and stopped there.

"Good morning," Meren said, and sat down on the floor beside her, which hurt.

Bea did not look up. She had drawn the same sign on the slate forty times, in chalk: a long stroke with a bend at the end of it.

"Read it to me again."

"Blood."

"Show me where the blood is."

Bea touched the bend at the end of the stroke. "Here. The hook turns back."

"And the dash?"

"What dash?"

Meren took the slate, wiped it with her sleeve, and gave it back. "Draw it as big as you can. As big as the floor."

The girl drew it as big as the floor. The long stroke. The hook at the end, turning back toward the stem. And across the stem, near the bottom, the small bar that she had told Meren about in her second week, the bar that meant *water*, the bar that nobody drew any more.

"The dash is what makes it water," Meren said. "You told me that yourself. In the spring."

Bea looked at the floor, and then at the leaves, and then at the floor again. The leaves had the bar. They had had the bar for three days.

"It is not blood," she said. "It is iron in the water."

"Say it again."

"It is iron. In the water." Her voice did not break, which was the worst part. She had been raised on this farm and she knew exactly what she had just said out loud. "It has the dash. It has the dash and I did not see it because the chart does not have it."

Meren stayed on the floor a moment longer with her hands on her knees, doing the arithmetic of three days: a palm, an arm, her own cut, and the gauze still rolled on the shelf.

"The chart," she said. "Who drew the chart?"

"The Reader before me. He read for eleven years." Bea was crying now, in the way children cry when they have not yet been told whether they are in trouble. "He lost a tooth. He told me the chart was what he got instead of it."

Meren stood up. "Then here is what happens. You are going to read that sign out loud at the roots, in front of Rusk and everyone working the soil. I am going to take the feed line apart while you do it. And if the plant is still shaking at dusk, I open my own hand and Rusk writes whatever he likes in his book."

---

The crew came to the root wall the way the crew came when the plant shook: quietly, all of them, in work clothes, nobody sending for anybody.

Rusk brought the journal and opened it at a new page. Bea stood under the leaves with the slate against her chest and read the sign out loud — the long stroke, the hook, and the dash.

"*Iron in the water,*" she said.

Then she read it again, because Meren had told her to, and again: three times, one for each day.

Meren was already down in the feed trench with a wrench, on her knees in water that should have been clear. It was not clear. When she cracked the union at the manifold, what came out was brown, and it came out with grit in it. The valve seat was eaten through in a crescent, and the water had been running through that crescent into the root bed for months.

She had asked for a new seat twice. The manifest had struck it off twice.

"Open the bypass," she called up. "The clean one. All the way."

The brown water went off into the sump. Fresh water came down the line with a different sound, and the crew heard it through the floor.

The leaves held still for a moment. Then they moved — not the long slow tremor of the three days, but small and sideways, like a hand feeling along a wall. Then they stopped.

Nobody spoke. The boy from the water crew said, "Is it done?" and Meren, still in the trench, said, "It is done."

---

They bandaged the three arms before the light went.

The woman from the second shift said nothing at all. The boy watched his own hand being wrapped as if it belonged to somebody on the surface. Rusk stood by the journal and touched his jaw, twice, on the side where the tooth would have come from, and did not know that Meren was watching him do it.

Then he wrote in the green journal, at the top of the new page, the sentence that Meren dictated and Bea said out loud first, so that there could be no doubt about whose words they were:

*The plant asks for clean water in the roots. Nobody cuts until the child has read the sign at the roots and spoken it.*

The old chart came down off the wall. Meren folded it in four and put it in the drawer where she kept the things nobody wanted to look at.

"Nobody holds the bowl for me again, either," Bea said. "When there is cutting."

"There will not be cutting." Meren crouched to her, which hurt. "And that is a promise. Nobody opens a hand on a reading you have not spoken at the roots, in front of witnesses, with your own voice. Not next year, not when you are tall. Write it on your slate if you do not believe me."

Bea wrote it on her slate in chalk, in her round hand, and then drew the sign again underneath, freehand, not from the chart, from the leaves.

She stopped. She looked up at the wall.

"There is another one," she said.

---

The leaves above the roots had moved. High up, where the light was thin, a shape had come out that filled six leaves at once, with a stem that bent twice and a hook that turned all the way around.

Bea copied it with her chalk, and then, because she was seven and thorough, she turned the slate around so that the grown-ups could see it.

Rusk looked at it for a long time. Then he took the journal down again and turned it over to the back pages, where the Reader before Bea had written, nine years ago, one sign and one word beside it.

The same sign. The word: *flower*.

"Nine years," he said. "It is coming. The last time it flowered we gave it a name, and we have not said that name out loud since, and we never will."

Bea asked the question before anybody could stop her.

"Whose name this time?"
