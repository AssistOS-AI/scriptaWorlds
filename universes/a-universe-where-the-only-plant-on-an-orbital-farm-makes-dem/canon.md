# Canon — Hesper, the farm with a single plant

## Fundamental laws
- The farm's plant speaks: not with a voice, but through leaves, which twist into signs that children read better than old people.
- Whatever the plant asks is granted: if it wants warmer water the whole farm warms; if it wants blood, someone cuts his hand over the roots and is not bandaged until the plant stops shaking.
- Requests are written in the green journal, and anyone who fails them within three days wakes without a tooth, then without a finger, then without an eye; no one has ever reached the fourth day.
- The plant may not know about other plants, so stories of forests and fields are forbidden, and those who tell them are set to work in silence at the roots.
- Once every nine years the plant flowers and then asks for a new name: the crew gives it the name of a dead child, and that child is never mentioned again on the farm, so as not to offend the flower.
- Established consequence: a request counts as met only when the plant stops shaking, so the crew measures blood by the trembling and not by any amount, and cuts stay unbandaged for as long as the shaking lasts.
- Established consequence: the signs are drawn shapes, not words, so one shape can carry two meanings and the difference may be a single stroke; an adult who has learned to split shapes into letters reads only the strokes he was taught.
- Established consequence: the plant's current name is the name of a dead child of Hesper, so nobody on the farm says it out loud; the crew says "the plant" and the child is not spoken of at all.
- Established consequence: a line written in the green journal is a debt of the person who wrote it; the count runs out at the end of the third day, and the price is taken in the night, so that the writer wakes on the next morning without a tooth.
- Established consequence: the plant has never answered anything written in the green journal; it answers only what is done to it, and in chapter 2 it stopped shaking for light while a written line about blood stood against it.
- Established consequence: the price of a failed request is paid once and then the count starts again, and nobody on Hesper has ever let a request stand unpaid past that first price, which is why the crew says there has never been a fourth day.
- Established consequence: the ladder of prices runs on the person and not on the request: an officer who wrote one unmet line lost a tooth, and when he wrote a second unmet line the same hand paid the next price, an index finger, which nobody on Hesper had ever paid before.
- Established consequence: the plant does not perceive writing at all; the open green journal held up at the wall moves no leaf, and a hand lamp held up in the same place turns the whole crown.
- Established consequence: the price is taken from whoever wrote the line and never from whoever offered to cut, so a written line is a debt of one man and the farm stands outside it.
- Established consequence: once a price has been taken the count runs again on the same unmet request, so a request that nobody can answer can cost three prices, and what follows an eye is not known on Hesper.
- Established consequence: the price comes off the hand the line belongs to; nine years ago the Reader wrote his own reading in the green journal and the book took his tooth, and since chapter 2 the officer writes the book and signs it, so the debt of a reading now falls on the officer.
- Established consequence: the count is charged against the request that stands under a line, so a new line written over the same sign closes the count of the old one; in chapter 5 the name request was paid in blood on the day the officer wrote his fourth line, and on the third morning no price was taken.
- Established consequence: a line can be written so that it is already kept, because a request counts as met the moment the plant is still, and the plant has been still since the crown lamps came on; the officer's fourth line promised blood until the plant is still, so it was finished the moment the pen left the page.
- Established consequence: the book speaks to the crew and never to the plant, so an officer's wording is carried out by the people who read it, and the plant answers only the act; in chapter 5 the crown and the open flower turned down toward the cut hand over the soil as they had turned toward a hand lamp.
- Established consequence: the plant turns toward what it lacks and not toward what it is told, so after the blood sank into the root bed the open flower kept its face down to the trench and stopped attending to the crown lamps it had followed since chapter 2.
- Established consequence: the three days are the farm's law and not the plant's count, because the plant counts nothing and keeps no calendar; a number in a sign is read by the hand that writes the line, never by the plant.
- Established consequence: the book does not divide a request from a promise — a line is a debt of the hand that wrote it, and nine years ago the Reader's line that the farm would keep the child it had named failed within three days, because the farm then took her name off the register page, the slate and the walls.
- Established consequence: a name given to the flowering is a debt of memory and not of paper, so from chapter 6 a name may be given only by a hand that remembers the child, said out loud at the roots once and never written down.

- Established consequence: the book closes counts and cannot answer a plant, so the farm runs two instruments on two different subjects: in chapter 7 eight days of lines, including the officer's sixth line held open both at the wall and at the flower's own aim, moved no leaf, while a quarter turn of the wheel at the root line turned the low leaves, the crown and the open flower together.
- Established consequence: the ladder of prices has a top and the farm has never looked past it; in chapter 7 the officer paid the third price, an eye, for his sixth line, the line still stood, and the count restarted with nothing on the farm's ladder left to charge.
- Established consequence: a count is closed by a new line written over the same sign and never by anything the plant does, because the plant keeps no count at all and reads no page.
- Established consequence: what the farm calls the plant's answer is what its own instrument measures; in chapter 7 the work ring's gauge moved a tenth for the first time in nine days when the root line was opened a quarter turn, and it had not moved through the whole week in which the farm wrote, bled and named.
- Established consequence: the law charges lines and never rules, so a rule the crew makes at the wall has no price of its own; in chapter 8 the officer entered the farm's seventh name in the green journal against the crew's rule of the fifteenth day and against his own sixth line, and nothing was taken from him, because the count runs on a line that is not kept and on nothing else.
- Established consequence: a new line written over the same sign takes the charge off the line above it and leaves that line standing, so the way off the ladder is a pen and not a body; in chapter 8 the officer's seventh line closed the count of his sixth with one sentence, and both lines stand on the page, one above the other.
- Established consequence: a written name is a name the farm can keep without saying it, because writing is not speech; from chapter 8 the seventh name stands in the book, no mouth on Hesper has said it twice, and the law charged nothing for the page either.
- Established consequence: the plant's asking ends with its own body and not with the farm's answer, so a request that the farm never answers can end by itself; in chapter 8 the flower closed and the sign came apart leaf by leaf on a morning when no leaf had moved for the book, and the farm read the quiet wall as the pen's doing.
- Established consequence: the law charges lines and never the rules a farm makes for itself, so a rule made at the wall with no line under it costs nothing to make and nothing to break; in chapter 9 the ring made Hesper's whole rule of choosing at the root wall and left it out of the green journal on purpose.
- Established consequence: a name read off a page is not a name a hand is holding, because the register carries hundreds of dead children whom no mouth at the wall can put a face to; in chapter 9 the ring read those pages out and found that the farm can still see two children and both of them are already the plant's.
- Established consequence: the plant asks the farm for nothing while its own body already has what it lacked, so a quiet wall is not a request; in chapter 9 the child said out loud that the small shape at the root line is turned at the water and not at the ring, and the farm answered it with the wheel and the number.
- Established consequence: the farm's silence around a name it has given is the farm's rule and never the plant's price, because the law has charged nothing for a name said or written; in chapter 9 the mother of the last name asked the ring to say it at the wall once a year and no hand could answer her with a count.

## World
- Hesper is an orbital farm built as rings around a spine: the root ring at one end, where the plant grows, the work ring in the middle, where the crew lives and the infirmary stands, and the low ring, where the stores from the surface are kept.
- The plant is the only plant on Hesper; it fills the root wall and holds the station's air, which is why no request of its is ever argued about for long.
- The plant's root bed is fed from its own line, separate from the crew's water. The line runs from the manifold in the feed trench through a valve seat and into the soil.
- Hesper carries forty-one people. Food, gauze, medicines and spare parts come up from the surface on manifests, and a struck-off item does not come back.
- The green journal lies on the shelf at the root wall. The officer writes in it, one dated line per request, taken from a chart of drawn signs kept on the wall beside it.
- The Reader is a child of the farm; the post goes to the youngest child who can hold a slate. The Reader for the last nine years has been adults followed by children, and the chart on the wall was drawn by the adult who read before Bea.
- The crown of the root wall is the darkest part of the ring. Two lamp arms with bowl-shaped shades hang over it, and they had been dark for two years because their ballast was struck off the manifest.
- The work ring's lamp arms use the same fittings as the crown brackets and can be unbolted and carried up the spine; all of the farm's lamps draw on one power budget, and the officer signs the draw in the log.
- The infirmary lamp is one of the work ring arms, so light for the crown is light taken from the doctor.
- Since the flowering began, the air gauge of the work ring moves with the plant's strain: it lost a tenth of a point when the flower sign came up and another tenth while the request stood unmet, and it climbed again once the crown lamps burned.
- Meren keeps a column of numbers on the infirmary wall: the daily air reading of the work ring.
- The sign of this flowering's name request is the flower sign with three hooks under it, and it has no entry in any chart on the farm.
- Since the crown lamps came on, the crown has leaned into the two bowl shades without stopping and the open flower has kept its face turned to that light through every argument at the root wall.
- The officer of Hesper knows the name given to the plant at the last flowering, because he wrote the entry nine years ago, and nobody on the farm has said it since.
- The green journal stands on the shelf at the root wall and is read by the crew every morning the way the gauge is read; from chapter 3 it holds a line the officer wrote and could not carry out.
- The low ring holds the farm's register, where every birth and every death on Hesper is written on the page of its year.
- The name the plant carries was taken off that register page, off the dead child's slate and off every wall of the farm nine years ago, and Iven did the taking.
- The woman from the second shift is the mother of the child whose name the plant carries; she has worked the second shift for nine years and does not stand under the flower.
- The old chart came down to the stores in Meren's pocket in chapter 4 and lies folded open on a crate there, and Iven folded every word under its own sign before the lesson so that the shapes were left alone on the paper.
- Four signs on the leaf wall have no entry in the old chart.
- The cuts of the ritual are made over the bare soil of the root bed in the trench, and the knife for them hangs on a hook beside the shelf at the root wall.
- From chapter 5 the green journal carries four lines written by Rusk, and the fourth one stands under the name sign and has no price against it.
- Since chapter 2 the crown lamps have burned while the work ring stayed dark, and from chapter 5 the open flower no longer holds its face to them.
- The gauge column on the infirmary wall is the doctor's own instrument; from chapter 5 it is the only record on Hesper of whether the plant is being answered at all.
- The register of the low ring keeps a page for every year of the farm, with its births and its deaths in the hand of whoever kept it then; the page of a flowering year is cut out at the binding when the name is given, and a stub a finger wide is left in the book.
- Six flowering years had left six stubs in the register before chapter 6, and this flowering made the seventh, so the farm's record of what it has spent on the plant is a set of holes, and each hole is a whole year of births, deaths and ring readings and not only one name.
- The root line comes up out of the manifold and into the soil at a seat in the trench; from chapter 6 the open flower's face is turned at that seat rather than at the patch of soil where the four hands bled.
- Verrin is the oldest hand on Hesper and works the trench and the root bed; she was seven in the year a crate came off the rack in the spine and killed the girl whose bunk was above hers.

- The bypass wheel that Meren opened in the spring stands beside the seat where the root line comes up out of the manifold into the soil, within reach of a hand standing in the trench.
- Meren's column on the infirmary wall carries the same number for nine days of the flowering, and then, on the day the wheel was turned, a number a tenth higher.
- The leaf wall is the plant's only instrument of asking, so a wall of flat unturned leaves is a plant that is asking for nothing; before chapter 8 the farm had never seen the wall at rest under an open flowering.
- From the twenty-first day of the flowering the wall stands quiet, with no sign over the shelf, and the farm has no reading to make and no request to answer for the first time in the book.
- The small new shape that came up in the leaves along the root line at the seat is no bigger than a thumbprint, it is turned at the water, and it has no entry in any chart on the farm.
- From the twenty-second day the root line is read at the seat before every shift and the reading is written into the doctor's column, so the farm's first duty to the plant is clean water and not a reading of leaves.
- The flower lies closed on the seat where the root line comes up out of the manifold, the crown lamps burn above it, and the wall has stood flat and unturned since the twenty-first morning.
- The small shape in the leaves along the root line has not grown since the wall went quiet, and it stands turned at the water every morning.
- The register was carried up to the root wall on the twenty-second day and read out page by page: seven stubs, and hundreds of names of dead children with no face behind them in the ring.
- The farm keeps one day a year for its own dead — the day the flowering began — at the root wall, at the shift bell, with the names said aloud and never read off a page. The first keeping stood empty on the twenty-second day.

## Recurring characters
- **Doctor Meren** — the physician of Hesper, forty-four, nineteen years on the farm, with two refused requisitions for a valve seat behind her. She cannot read leaf signs because she is an adult, and she treats the crew's arms as the farm's real budget. In chapter 1 she refused the third cut, bet her own hand on the child's reading, cracked the feed line open, and dictated the new journal rule. She promised Bea that no hand will be opened on a reading Bea has not spoken aloud. In chapter 2 she held that promise in front of the crew when the boy from the water crew offered his hand, read the air gauge as the plant's condition, and took her own infirmary lamp down to give the crown its light. In chapter 3 she said in front of the crew that no child has died on Hesper in nine years and that there is no name to give, put the price of a second unmet line in the middle of the ring, wrote the same gauge number down three times, and refused the proposal to close the green journal because the law's own first line is that requests are written in it. In chapter 4 she took Bea and the folded chart down to the stores and made the old Reader teach the child, stood over her while she practised saying out loud that she does not know what the hooks ask, asked Iven whose name was given nine years ago and how it was chosen, and heard the answer from the woman from the second shift instead. In chapter 5 she tried to stop the officer from writing over the child's reading, read his new line twice and named what it was — a page that can always be paid — bandaged four cut hands on the spot because the plant had been still for days and the old rite had no clock left in it, and put the farm's real measure in the middle of the ring: when her gauge moves, the farm will find out what it answered. In chapter 6 she made the child read the whole sign in front of the ring before anything was spent, made the farm's first rule of choosing — a name may be given to the plant only by a hand that remembers the child, said out loud at the roots once, never by the officer, the child or the book — took the voice of the naming out of the officer's mouth in front of the crew because his own line had already failed at the roots, and wrote the morning number at the foot of her column after the name was said: the same number as the day before. In chapter 7 she told the officer at the shelf that the rule the ring made at the wall does not need his pen, offered him the only way out of the count — write a new line over the sixth one and the count is charged on the new line, as he had done in the spring — and heard him refuse it to keep the record; on the third morning she found nothing on his eye to dress, because a price takes nothing that can be dressed, gave the ring the arithmetic of a ladder with no rung above an eye, and then wrote the first new number in nine days at the foot of her column, a tenth higher, after a quarter turn of the wheel at the seat. In chapter 8 she told the officer the book has never moved anything and went to her own instrument instead: she demanded the name be read out loud for the ring and was refused, sent the boy to open the bypass wheel another quarter turn at the seat on the twentieth day, and wrote a second tenth into her column, so both movements in it now come off a wheel; on the twenty-first morning there was nothing on the officer to dress because the count had been closed by a pen, and she said to the ring that she had two numbers and no story to go with them. In chapter 9 she put the root line and her column in the place of a reading, refused the water crew's page in front of the ring, and put the keeping in plain words: one day a year at the wall, the farm's dead said out loud and never read off a page.
- **Officer Rusk** — sixty, the officer of Hesper, who writes the green journal. He told a story about a forest when he was nine, worked a year at the roots in silence for it, and still says "the place in the picture" instead of the word. He offered his own arm on the third day rather than change the wording of a request. In chapter 1 he accepted the child's reading as a test, wrote the new rule, and found the flowering sign in the back of the journal. In chapter 2 he read the new shape from the chart in his head as blood again, wrote the adult line in the green journal with his own name under it, took the debt on himself, moved the work ring's lamp arms to the crown, and woke on the fourth morning without his left front tooth. In chapter 3 he fought the reader's rule because the book could hold the plant's questions and no answer of the farm's, wrote a third line promising that the farm would answer with the name it has and that he would say it at the roots on the third morning, stood at the roots in front of the crew and could not say it, and woke on the fourth morning without the index finger of his left hand; the count has restarted on him and the next price is an eye. In chapter 4 he wrote no new line, said that the line promising the farm's answer is his and stays in the book, and agreed that nothing goes on that page until the child has read the whole sign in the light. In chapter 5, on the morning before the count reached his eye, he broke the rule he had made and wrote an adult wording from his memory of the old chart — the stroke under the sign, blood over the roots until the plant is still — cut his own left palm open over the root bed so that the page would have a hand behind it and not only a pen, said out loud in front of the crew that the child was right about the lamp and that it would not matter, and kept the eye, because the line he had written was finished before he cut. In chapter 6 he called the farm to the root wall for the first choosing Hesper has ever held, said in front of the ring that the book has four lines in it and no line for a choosing, gave up the voice at the roots that his spring line had promised when the doctor told him the farm had not let that line go, and wrote and signed the naming entry so that it asks for nothing. In chapter 7 he said in front of the ring that the farm's first choosing stood in one old woman's memory and nowhere in the book, wrote his sixth line — the name said at the roots on the fifteenth day is to be entered under the sign by the officer before the count — asked Verrin for the name and was refused, refused in turn to close the count by writing over the line because that would strike the choosing out of the record, woke on the third morning without his left eye, the first third price on Hesper, held the book open where the flower was aimed and moved no leaf, and nodded to the boy to turn the wheel at the seat. In chapter 8, with the count running toward a price nobody could name, he wrote his seventh line over the same sign — the name said at the roots on the fifteenth day entered by the officer's own hand from his own hearing — put the two sounds of the name into the middle of the entry, refused to let the doctor read the name out loud because a written name is a name nobody has to say, closed the count of his sixth line with that one sentence, and kept the rest of his face on the third morning; he then stood in the trench at the seat with the book under his arm and did not say whether he believed the ring or the doctor about the quiet wall. In chapter 9 he put the choosing to the ring himself and closed the book without writing: the farm's rule of choosing stands in no line of the green journal, and the officer who bought the farm nine years with a page is now the man who refuses the pen.
- **Bea** — seven years old, the Reader of Hesper for eleven months, the one who reads the leaves. She read *blood* for three days from the old Reader's chart, then found the missing dash on the leaves and said *iron in the water* out loud at the roots. She cannot read written words well. She was promised by Meren that no cut will follow a reading she has not spoken herself. In chapter 2 she refused to read the new shape until she knew it, asked for a hand lamp, and read it by watching the crown leaves turn toward the lamp: the two bowls over the crown, filled. In chapter 3 she read the new sign aloud as *give me a name* and said out loud that the three hooks have no chart entry she knows, held the open green journal up at the wall and then a hand lamp, which showed the farm that the plant answers light and nothing else, and insisted on standing in the ring on the third morning because the Reader has to hear the name. In chapter 4 she learned from Iven to read the shape first and the word after, said out loud that a word could be wrong although she had no right one, called her own chapter 3 reading a half reading because she read the top of the sign and what the farm remembered and not the end of it, practised refusing a reading in front of the waiting crew, and went down the spine alone in the evening to make Iven draw the sign of the last flowering. In chapter 5 she read the sign again for the officer in the same three parts and said again that she did not know what the hooks ask, and then watched the plant turn for a cut hand and said in front of the ring that it turned the same way every time a lamp was moved along the wall; the doctor said she was right and the crew cut four hands anyway. In chapter 6 she went down to the stores without the doctor and without the book, heard what Iven wrote nine years ago and why the book took his tooth, read the end of the name sign in front of the crew as the tip of the blood sign turned back on itself three times, and said out loud that the plant counts nothing and the three days are the farm's; she sat on the rail when the name was said and was the only one on the farm who looked where the flower was looking. In chapter 7 she said out loud at the rail what she had seen on the fifteenth day: that the flower had never looked at the page but at the seat, and the farm proved her right within the hour when a quarter turn of the wheel at the root line lifted the open flower off the trench. In chapter 8 she stood on the rail under a wall that was taking its sign apart, said out loud that the plant had not answered and had stopped asking while the ring read the quiet as the pen's work, and then went down into the trench and found the small new shape come up in the leaves along the root line, which she does not know. In chapter 9 she read that shape as turned at the water and not at the ring, was told by Verrin at the roots that a name was spent on the fifteenth day and will never be given to her, and asked for the farm's dead to be said to her from now on, so that she is the first mouth of the keeping.
- **Iven** — the Reader before Bea; he read the leaves for eleven years, misread a sign nine years ago and lost a tooth, and drew the chart of signs that replaced his reading. He works the stores in the low ring and does not read any more. In chapter 2 he told Rusk that a tooth leaves no pain and no blood, and that the chart was what he did with the rest of that year. In chapter 3 he came up from the low ring to tell Rusk that the green journal is a chart like his own, that the plant will not notice a word written on it, and that the crew would read a failure at the roots as a refusal by the plant. In chapter 4 he refused to teach the chart and taught the shape instead, folded every word under its sign, hid the end of a sign behind his thumb so that the child had to say she did not know, told her that a shape can tell you no, admitted that the line of nine years ago was his own writing and that the book took his tooth for it, refused to say how the name was chosen or to put it in the child's mouth, and drew the sign of the last flowering on a crate with no hooks at the end of it; he then promised her the signs he has not drawn yet, alone, without the doctor and without the book. In chapter 6 he kept that promise and drew the two signs he read — the one from eighteen years ago, which ended in a single hook, and the one from nine years ago, which ended in nothing — came up the spine with the crate to tell the ring that nine years ago there was no choosing, only a hand on a page, told the child what his own line of that year promised and why the book took his tooth, and carried the register down the spine to cut the year's page out of it with the others. In chapter 8 he is not in the ring: the officer's seventh line does what Iven's own line of nine years ago could not, which is to put a chosen name into the book in the officer's writing, and the old Reader has not yet been asked what he thinks of a page that keeps a name by entering it. In chapter 9 he carried the register up to the wall himself and said in front of the ring that a mouth can be trained onto a page, which the child answered by saying that is not reading but saying.
- **The woman from the second shift** — the crew member who opened her palm in the spring and held it open for three days, who said in chapter 3 that she would not be in the ring when the name was said, and who answered Meren in chapter 4 that the name the plant carries is her daughter's, that her daughter was seven, and that she has worked the second shift for nine years so as not to stand under the flower. In chapter 5 she refused to cut with the rest, said that nine years ago the book had a line like the officer's and the farm paid it with a name while this year it was paying with hands, and asked the shelf what any of it was buying. In chapter 6 she refused the cheap name in front of the crew — a name no hand holds is paper, and a farm that spends one learns that lesson a second time — and then stood at the rail through a naming for the first time in nine years, from the first word to the last. In chapter 7 she asked the officer in front of the ring what he had bought, now that the farm's seventh name had cost him an eye and was still written nowhere, and went back to her shift when he answered *time*. In chapter 8 she told him in front of the ring that the farm had put a name on a page for the first time in its history, and that the one hand which could say whether he had heard it right had already refused; after the wall went quiet she told him what nine years of silence would cost, that the next flowering would read the name off his page with nobody left in the ring who ever heard her. In chapter 9 she counted the farm's two remembered children aloud in front of the ring — hers and Verrin's, both spent — and then asked the officer to put her daughter's name into the keeping once a year, which is the one name the fundamental law forbids the farm to mention again.
- **The boy from the water crew** — fourteen, twice refused a place in the water crew, praised in chapter 1 for the cut he offered; he offered his hand again in chapter 2 and Meren refused it. In chapter 3 he said out loud that somebody has to answer the plant and that is what an officer is for. In chapter 5 he was the first over the rail after the officer opened his palm, said that the plant was drinking and that everyone had seen it turn, and carried the crew's certainty about the blood further than any adult on the farm. In chapter 6 he gave the ring the cheap answer at the wall: take the oldest name in the register, the one no hand remembers, and let the plant have a word out of a book. In chapter 7 he took the book off the shelf himself and held it open at the wall to test the line against the plant, got no leaf for it, and later that day turned the bypass wheel a quarter turn at the seat, which turned the low leaves, lifted the open flower off the trench and put a tenth of a point back into the work ring's air. In chapter 8 he held up the officer's new line as an answer and lost the argument to the doctor's two numbers, and after the flower closed he said out loud in front of the ring that the plant had read the written name that morning; he is still on the water crew, still with the bandaged hand, and the only hand on Hesper whose work has ever moved the gauge. In chapter 9 he argued for a name taken off the register page, was answered by the doctor's two numbers and the officer's face, and holds the wheel at the seat before every shift.
- **Verrin** — the oldest hand on Hesper, who works the trench and the root bed and had never spoken at the wall before chapter 6. She was seven in the year a crate came off the rack in the spine and killed the girl whose bunk was above hers, and she is the only hand on the farm who still remembers a child named on the register's surviving pages. In chapter 6 she put her hand on the register, gave that child to the flowering, said the name once over the bare soil of the trench in front of the whole ring, and set three terms for the price: the register's holes stay open and the number of names spent stands in the green journal beside the farm's days, the Reader is told that a name was spent and will never be given it, and the saying is done at the roots and not at the shelf. In chapter 7 she refused the officer's request to say the name a second time so that it could be entered in the book, told him that the line was his debt and not hers and that no hand of hers was under it, and stayed at the root bed while the count took his eye. In chapter 8 she would not read the officer's seventh line and would not say the name a second time to check his ear, and told him in front of the rail that what he wrote down is what the farm will read in nine years, when the hand that reads it never heard her at all; her third term, that the name is said at the roots and never written, was broken by his pen and the law charged nothing for it. In chapter 9 she said the rule of the choosing in front of the ring, with the register open beside her, and paid the last term of her own bargain in the trench by telling the child that a name was spent at the roots and will never be given to her.

## Timeline
- Nine years ago — the plant flowered; the crew gave it the name of a child who had died on the farm that year, and that name has not been spoken on Hesper since.
- Nine years ago — the Reader before Bea misread a sign, lost a tooth, left the post and drew the chart of signs that replaced his reading; he works in the low ring and does not read any more.
- Nine years ago — the last funeral on Hesper: no one has died on the farm since, which is why no dead child is available to name the flower.
- Chapter 1, day 1 to day 3 — the plant asks for blood three mornings running and does not stop shaking; three crew members bleed and stay unbandaged.
- Chapter 1, day 3, afternoon — Bea reads the sign from the leaves as iron in the water; Meren opens the feed line, finds the eaten valve seat and rust, opens the clean bypass, and the plant stops shaking before dusk. Nobody loses a tooth and the three arms are bandaged.
- Chapter 1, day 3, dusk — the plant shows the flowering sign for the first time in nine years, and Bea asks whose name the crew will give it.
- Chapter 2, day 4 — the buds open, the air gauge loses a tenth, and a new small shape appears under the flower sign; Bea refuses to read it, and Rusk writes an adult line for blood again and puts his own name under it.

- Chapter 8, day 21, third morning — no price is taken and the officer keeps what is left of his face, because his seventh line closed the count of the sixth; in the middle of the same morning the flower closes, and the sign with the three hooks comes apart leaf by leaf, the hooks last, leaving the wall flat and unturned; the ring reads the quiet wall as the plant accepting the written name, Meren says she has two numbers and both of them came off a wheel, and Bea says out loud that the plant did not answer and stopped asking.
- Chapter 8, day 21, the trench — the shift starts late; Rusk stands at the seat where the water comes in and Bea finds a small new shape come up in the leaves along the root line, no bigger than a thumbprint and turned at the water, and says she does not know it.
- Chapter 9, day 22, the root wall — the wall is flat and the ring comes up anyway; Meren puts the root line and her column in the place of a reading, with the wheel read at the seat before every shift, and the child says out loud that the small shape at the water is turned at the water and not at the ring, so the plant is asking the farm for nothing.
- Chapter 9, day 22, at the wall — the officer opens the choosing while no sign is up and the law can charge nothing for it; the register is carried up and read out, the ring finds it can still see two children and both of them are spent, and the water crew's answer — a name taken off the page — is met by the doctor's two numbers and the officer's face.
- Chapter 9, day 22, the rule — the ring makes the rule of the choosing at the wall, in front of the register: it is done in the open, a hand may offer a child it knew alive, no hand is ever asked, any hand may refuse an offering, and the offering hand says the name at the roots once; the officer writes nothing, so the rule stands in no line of the green journal.
- Chapter 9, day 22, the keeping — the farm makes one day a year for its own dead, at the wall, at the shift bell, with the names said aloud and never read off a page; the first keeping stands empty in the same morning, because every child the farm can still name is the plant's.
- Chapter 9, day 22, the trench — Verrin pays the last term of her bargain and tells the child at the roots that a name was spent on the fifteenth day, that it is in no page the child can read, and that it will never be given to her; the child asks for the farm's dead to be said to her from now on, and the keeping has its first mouth.
- Chapter 9, day 22, the rail — the woman from the second shift asks the officer to put her daughter's name into the keeping once a year, which is the one name the fundamental law forbids the farm to mention again; the officer does not answer her, and the ring comes up for the second shift.

## Stable facts
- A request is met when the plant stops shaking; there is no recorded amount of anything, no dose and no measure but the trembling.
- Three days of blood changed nothing: the leaves shook through every cut, and stopped only for a few minutes after each one, which is why the crew believed the blood was working.
- The sign for blood and the sign for iron in the water are the same long stroke with a bend at the end; blood ends with the hook turned back toward the stem, and iron carries a short dash across the stem, the dash that means water.
- Children read the leaf shapes whole; adults read the strokes they were taught and stop there. This is why the Reader is a child.
- The old chart of signs on the root wall was drawn by an adult and has no dash on the blood sign.
- Meren requisitioned a new valve seat twice; the surface manifest struck it off both times, and the brown water ran into the root bed for months.
- Since chapter 1, the officer writes the reader's own words into the green journal, no one cuts until the child has read the sign at the roots and spoken it, and the old chart has been taken off the wall.
- The old chart lies folded in four in Meren's drawer; Rusk writes from his memory of it, which is thirty years deep.
- The chart's mark for a request that comes back is a bar set above the sign, and that bar is why Rusk read the new small shape as blood again.
- The new small shape of the flowering is a long stroke with two hooks turned toward each other at the top, and it means the two bowl shades of the crown lamps held up and filled: the plant was asking for light on the crown.
- A child can read a shape by testing it against the plant's own body: when Bea held a hand lamp to the wall, every leaf of the crown turned to it, and when she took the lamp away they turned back to the dark.
- An officer who wants a line written writes it with his own name under it; Rusk was the first to write his name in the green journal.
- A tooth taken by the law leaves no pain, no blood and a closed gum; Iven lost one nine years ago and Rusk lost his left front tooth on the morning after the count ran out.
- The crown lamps burn, and the work ring is dark for the shift in which its arms were carried up the spine; the farm's power margin is spent on the crown.
- Nobody on Hesper says the word for a forest or a field; the punishment is a year of silent work at the roots, and it is still served.
- The plant's name from the last flowering is never spoken, because saying it would name the dead child.
- A line in the green journal that does not come true within three days costs its writer one price from the ladder tooth, then finger, then eye, and the price never falls on the farm and never on whoever offered to cut.
- Rusk wrote three lines in the green journal; the second and the third did not come true, and they cost him a tooth and an index finger, and the plant answered neither of them.
- Since the crown lamps came on the plant has not shaken once; the name sign stands without trembling, so the farm has no measure of whether that request is being answered.
- The green journal is not a channel to the plant: a slate, a page and the book itself move no leaf, and only light, water and warmth do.
- The farm's chapter 1 rule still stands for readings — the officer writes the reader's own words and no cut follows a reading the child has not spoken — but it says nothing about the officer's own answer to a request, and Rusk wrote that answer anyway.
- Nobody on Hesper knows who the count takes when no line names a payer, which is why the crew did not close the green journal.
- A reader can tell that a word is wrong without having the right one, and can say so: Iven taught Bea to read the shape first and the word after, with the chart's words folded under.
- Iven drew no sign on his chart that he was not sure of, and four signs on the leaf wall have no entry in it.
- Bea's chapter 3 reading of the name sign came from the flower sign at the top of it and from what the farm remembered of the last flowering; she read the top of the shape and not the end of it.
- Nine years ago the Reader wrote his own reading in the green journal, and the book took the price out of his tooth; the officer writes and signs the book now, and the name under a line is what the law charges.
- The name the plant carries is the daughter of the woman from the second shift; the register page is gone, the child's slate went down the spine with the stores, and no wall of the farm carries the name.
- The sign of the flowering nine years ago was the flower sign with the long stroke under it and nothing at the end of that stroke; this year's sign carries three hooks at the end.
- The chart gives the word *water* to a shape whose stem is forked; Bea read the fork and said the word was wrong, and Iven did not correct her.
- The plant has not shaken since the crown lamps came on, and the name sign has stood unchanged through every reading given to it.
- Rusk wrote a fourth line in the green journal on the morning the count reached an eye: the plant asking again as in the spring, blood over the roots unbandaged until the plant is still, and himself giving the first.
- The fourth line was kept the same morning it was written, because four hands were cut over the root bed before the shift ended, and no price was taken for it on the third morning.
- The fourth line could not fail: it promised blood until the plant is still, and the plant has been still since the crown lamps came on, so it was finished the moment the pen left the page; Meren said this out loud in front of the crew on the morning no price was taken.
- The turn of chapter 5 was the first time the farm saw the plant move for something a man did with his own body: the crown, the low leaves and the open flower came down toward the cut hand as they had come toward a hand lamp held by the child.
- Since chapter 5 the open flower holds its face down toward the trench where the blood went into the soil and no longer holds it toward the crown lamps, and the gauge of the work ring has not moved.
- The name request is closed on the page and not on the wall: the sign with the three hooks stands unchanged over the shelf while the book carries a blood line under it.
- The crew keeps the officer's wording over the child's reading; Meren and Bea said out loud that the plant turns for a lamp the same way it turned for a hand, and four hands were cut anyway.
- The green journal has no line for a choosing and never had one: before chapter 6 the officer read the sign, pointed at a page of the register and the Reader wrote the name that was on it, so every naming on Hesper was a hand on a page and not a choice.
- Nine years ago the farm gave the plant the only dead child of that year, so nobody chose her; the Reader's line that the farm would keep her failed because the farm then took her name off the register page, off the slate and off the walls, and the book took his tooth for it.
- A line in the green journal is a debt of its writer whether it asks for something or promises something; the book does not read intentions, and that is what makes a wording that asks for nothing the safest line an officer can write.
- The end of a leaf sign turned back toward its own stem is the mark the blood sign carries at its tip, where a thing goes back the way it came; the three hooks are that turn repeated, and the three days are the farm's law and not the plant's count.
- Eighteen years ago the flowering sign ended in one hook and the farm gave its name the same day; nine years ago the sign ended in nothing and the farm gave its name the same day; this year's three hooks have followed nothing the farm did.
- Hesper's chapter 6 rule of choosing: a name may be given to the plant only by a hand that remembers the child, said out loud at the roots once and never written down, never by the officer, the child or the book.
- The register's holes are counted at the wall and the number of names spent is written into the green journal with the naming line, so the farm's count of what it has spent stands beside the count of its own days: six pages were gone before this flowering and this one made seven.
- Every hole in the register is a whole year cut out at the binding, the births and the deaths of that year with the name, so the farm is missing six years of its own dead.
- The name given at this flowering exists only in Verrin's memory: it is not in the green journal, not in the register and on no wall of the farm, and the Reader is to be told that a name was spent and will never be given it.
- The plant did not move for the name said over the soil: not a leaf of the crown, not the low leaves and not the open flower, and the crown lamps burned on as they had all week.
- Since chapter 6 the plant carries the name of the child Verrin remembers, given at this flowering in place of the name it had carried for nine years.
- Since chapter 6 the open flower's face is aimed a hand's width past the patch of soil where the four hands bled, at the seat where the root line comes up out of the manifold into the soil, and the child was the first on the farm to look where it looks.

- Rusk has written six lines in the green journal: two that were kept, three that failed and were paid with a tooth, the first joint of an index finger and an eye, and one — the naming entry — that asks for nothing.
- The sixth line cannot be kept by the officer alone, because the name it requires is held only in Verrin's memory and she has refused to say it a second time; the line stands under the name sign with the count restarted on it.
- The farm's first rule of choosing is not written in the green journal: the book carries the officer's sixth line under the name sign and no line that records what the crew decided on the fifteenth day.
- A count is closed by a line written over the same sign, and the closing line is itself a debt of its writer; on the seventeenth day the officer knew this and refused it, because closing the count would have struck the farm's only record of its first choosing.
- The price ladder ends at an eye on Hesper: nobody has ever paid a fourth price and the farm has no record of what a fourth unmet charge takes.
- Since chapter 7 the officer of Hesper has one eye; the price left no pain and no blood, only a lid closed down over a smooth place.
- The plant does not attend to a page even when the page is held at the exact place its open flower is aimed: the book held open over the root-line seat moved no leaf at all.
- The plant attends to its root line: a quarter turn of the bypass wheel at the seat turned the low leaves, the crown and the open flower together, and lifted the flower off the trench for the first time in six days.
- The work ring's air gauge moved a tenth higher on the day the wheel was turned, which was the first movement since the flowering began, so the doctor's column is the only record on Hesper of what the farm has actually answered.
- A child can read an aim and not only a sign: Bea said at the rail that the flower had never looked at the page but at the seat, and the wheel proved her right within the hour.
- From chapter 8 the name given at this flowering stands in the green journal in the officer's hand, written from his own hearing of one saying at the roots; Verrin has refused to read the page or to say the name again, so no living hand on Hesper can confirm that the name in the book is the name the child had.
- This supersedes nothing but changes the reach of the old fact: before chapter 8 the farm's seventh name existed in one memory and on no page, and the officer paid an eye for that, and from chapter 8 it exists on a page and in no second memory.
- Hesper's chapter 6 rule of choosing — a name given only by a hand that remembers, said once at the roots and never written — was broken by the officer's pen on the nineteenth day of the flowering, in front of the whole crew, and no price was taken for it, because the rule is carried by no line in the green journal.
- Rusk has written seven lines in the green journal: two that were kept, three that failed and were paid with a tooth, the first joint of an index finger and an eye, one naming entry that asked for nothing, and the seventh, which entered the name and took the charge off the sixth line; the sixth and seventh lines both stand on the page, one above the other.
- The count was closed on the nineteenth day, so the third morning of the twenty-first brought no price and the officer kept what was left of his face; the farm still does not know what the law takes above an eye, because nothing was ever charged a fourth time.
- The officer's seventh line was finished the moment the pen left the page: it asked for nothing but what he did in writing it, so the count could not run on it and the pen is now the farm's only known way off the ladder.
- The crew's rule of choosing and the officer's sixth line now pull in opposite directions: the crew says the name may not be written, the book carries it in the officer's hand, and no line exists on either side that a count could be charged against.
- The farm's seventh name is written and unsaid: the book holds it, the register holds its hole, Verrin holds the only memory of the saying, and the child has been promised that she will be told a name was spent and never given it — a promise now made harder by the name lying in a book she stands beside every morning and cannot read.
- On the twenty-first day of the flowering the plant closed its flower and let the sign with the three hooks come apart leaf by leaf, the hooks last, and nothing was charged to anybody for it: no line of the farm was kept or broken by it and no hand on Hesper did anything to the plant that morning.
- The farm has two readings of the quiet wall and both are defensible from what it can see: the ring holds that the pen answered the plant and the flowering ended on the written name, while Meren points at a column with two movements in it, both following the wheel, and Bea holds that the plant did not answer and simply stopped asking.
- The work ring's gauge moved a second tenth on the twentieth day when the bypass wheel was opened another quarter turn, so the ballast of the farm's only measure of the plant is now two tenths, and both of them came from water.
- The wall has been quiet since the twenty-first day, and the farm went back to work without a sign to read, which has not happened in the book before.
- The two dead children Hesper can still put a face to are the girl who had the bunk above Verrin's, which the plant now carries, and the daughter of the woman from the second shift, whose name the farm may not say; the register's surviving pages hold hundreds of names of dead children with no face behind them in the ring.

## Mysteries with a fixed cause
- Why the plant asked for blood for three days without stopping — the real cause: it was never asking for blood. Its root water had carried rust and grit for months through an eaten valve seat, and the sign for iron in the water had been read as blood by adults working from an old chart. Hints given: the brown water in the trench, the two struck-off requisitions, the boy's hand praised for cutting, Bea's missing dash.
- Why the plant asks and what it wants with a crew at all — the real cause (fixed, not yet revealed in the text): the plant treats the farm as its water and its weather, and it has no idea that its signs are read as orders; it asks for what it lacks and stops shaking when it has it. Hints given: it stopped trembling when the water was clean, not when blood was given; it stopped trembling when the crown lamps came on, while a written line for blood still stood against it; a hand lamp moved in front of the wall made the whole crown follow it like a body following food; the open green journal held up at the wall moved no leaf, and the flower kept its face to the lamps through the whole of the officer's written answer; in chapter 4 the child read the whole sign end to end in the lamp light and the plant did not move for the reading either, while the shape drawn by the old Reader from nine years ago carried no hooks at all, which no want of the plant explains; and in chapter 5 the plant turned its crown and its open flower down to the wet soil under a cut hand, which the crew read as the plant wanting blood and which the doctor and the child read as the same turning it gave a lamp, and after that turn the flower kept its face to the trench and left the lamps; and in chapter 6 the farm said a name out loud over that same soil in front of the whole crew and the plant did not move by a leaf, and the child then saw that the flower's face is not on the patch of blood at all but a hand's width past it, at the seat where the root line comes up into the soil — the farm has obeyed the law twice at the roots and the plant has attended to neither; and in chapter 7 the farm held the open book up where the flower was aimed and moved nothing at all, while a quarter turn of the bypass wheel at that same seat turned the low leaves, the crown and the open flower together and put a tenth of a point back into the work ring's air — through nine days in which the farm wrote, bled and named, the only thing the plant attended to was its own water line. And in chapter 8 the plant closed its flower and took the sign off the wall altogether, leaf by leaf, on a morning when the officer's new line had moved nothing, and a second quarter turn of the wheel at the seat put a second tenth into the doctor's column — the farm read the quiet as an answer to the page, while the only two movements its instruments ever made came from water, and the asking ended by itself with nothing answered at all.
- Why children read the signs better than adults — the real cause (fixed, not yet revealed in the text): the signs are whole drawn shapes, and the farm's own teaching of letters is what breaks an adult's reading of them. Hints given: the old Reader's chart, Meren seeing a word where Bea saw a shape, the chart's bar above the sign that turned light into blood for the officer, Bea reading the new shape by the movement of the leaves rather than by any chart; the officer reading the name sign as a chart entry he could write a line about, while the child read the same sign as a request nobody could fulfil; in chapter 4 the old Reader folded every word under its sign and the child read one of them differently from the word he had written, and she named her own earlier reading as a reading of the top of the shape and of what the farm remembered; in chapter 6 she read the difference between three flowerings out of two chalk drawings and a wall at the other end of the spine, while the officer who had written a line about the same sign could not say what its end was, and the adult ring read the names of its own dead off a page without putting a face to one of them; and in chapter 7 the same child said at the rail that the flower had never looked at the page but at the seat, which the ring then proved by turning a wheel, while the officer who had written the line stood in the trench holding the book open over the exact place the flower was aimed and saw nothing move. And in chapter 8 the child was the only hand on the farm who read the end of the flowering correctly: while the ring took the closed flower and the flat wall for an answer to the officer's page, she said out loud that the plant had not answered and had stopped asking, and then she was the one who found the small new shape come up at the root line — the officer, who had spent the morning being proved right by the wall, walked past that shape on his way to the seat.
- What the three hooks under the flower sign count — the real cause (fixed, not yet revealed in the text): the plant does not count and keeps no calendar, the hooks are part of one whole drawn shape, and the farm's own three-day law is what makes it read a number into them.
