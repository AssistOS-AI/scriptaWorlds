# Who Pays for the Quiet

The workshop has five windows and all five are blind.

They were glazed opaque in the winter, after the light off the low fields made the small ones squint at their work. Nobody objected. The gardener who used to wave up at them from the water lines objected a little, and she was written on the account anyway, because in this sector nothing is solved before somebody has said who pays for it.

Aster sits on the sill of the third window with her cheek against the cold band where the glass went blind.

She is eleven and she feels absences the way other people feel weather. A missing tooth she can find with her tongue. A room that has just been left. The two days last spring when the workshop's oldest student went up to the upper floor and did not come back down: she had felt that one for a week, a draught along the back of her neck, wherever she stood.

The blind windows she likes. They are honest absences. Everyone agreed to them and everyone can point at them.

Vela comes in with the licence form.

"You said the board would move it," she says. "It didn't move."

Aster turns from the glass. The board is a long slate by the door, and the quiet room test is still written on the afternoon line in Odile's flat hand. Above it, the licence number, and beside the number the small red mark the Ledger puts on a thing it has sent back.

"Show me."

Vela holds the form out, not quite giving it to her. The children drafted it in the workshop's own words, the way they draft everything, and the Ledger wrote back in its own.

Under *who pays*, in Vela's handwriting: *Odile. She loses one student she can read.*

Under that, in a clerk's careful script: *Refused. The price must be borne by the payer, and the payer must consent to it in their own voice.*

"I don't see the problem," Vela says. "It's true. She reads everything. She reads the kettles, she reads who slept badly, she reads the water lines. Take one of us out of her reading and she pays. She'll say yes. She'd say it today."

"She doesn't pay much," Aster says.

"She pays a lot."

"She pays in what she has most of." Aster slides down off the sill. "You pay in something you haven't got a spare of."

They have been circling this since breakfast, and this is the closest they have come to saying it.

At breakfast Aster had read the transfer list out loud off the open ledger, the way she reads everything out loud, because nothing here is closed and she has never once thought of reading as a thing that could be done quietly. Vela's name was on the list, fifth line, upper workshop, end of season. Vela had not told anyone. Everyone looked at Vela instead of at their plates, and Vela left the table, and the morning has been like a room with the air let out of it.

"I was going to tell you," Vela says now.

"I know."

"I was going to tell you today."

"I know," Aster says. "I don't mind that. I mind that I heard it from the list and not from you, and the list is readable, so it wasn't even a secret. It was just late."

"It *was* a secret," Vela says. "It was mine to say."

That is the whole argument, in one sentence, and neither of them has a second one.

---

Odile comes at midday and sits down on the floor with the form, which is how she reads everything, low, with her elbows on her knees.

Odile keeps this sector. She is not a god and she does not pretend to be. She knows the kettles and the water lines and the sleeping charts and every ledger in the workshop, and she knows the children the way a woman knows a house she built, which is very well and from the outside. She can read almost every system under her roof.

Almost: the children have spent four months on the thing that is not under her roof, and she has watched them do it, and she has signed their reading hours and lent them the thermometers.

"This is a good refusal," she says, tapping the clerk's line. "The Ledger isn't saying your reasoning is wrong. It is saying it is incomplete. There is a payer you haven't written down."

"There isn't another one," Vela says.

Odile looks at Aster, and Aster looks at the floor, and the afternoon decides itself.

"It's me," Aster says. "She hides an hour of herself, and I feel the hole. Wherever I am standing when it's made. For as long as it's there."

Nobody says anything for a moment.

"So write me down," Aster says. "I consent."

"You can't," Odile says, gently. "Not yet. That's the entire problem with the form."

---

The room is not built. It is subtracted.

The workshop has thirty-one rooms and always exactly thirty-one, because the sector is finite and stays finite; you do not add a room here, you take a room that was going to exist anyway and give it to nobody. Odile showed them the trick in the spring and the children understood it faster than she did. That is what the four months have been for: not a new place, but a place with the record taken out.

Vela signs first as the one who hides. Odile signs under her as the one who loses a student's hour out of her reading, and does not say anything warm about it, because the form has no line for that.

Then Vela walks into the small room off the drying floor, which is a room now and was a wall this morning, and the door closes, and the hour begins.

Aster puts her hand on the door.

She has been told what to expect. It arrives anyway. It is not cold exactly; it is the feeling of standing at the edge of a bed that has been stripped, and knowing precisely how long the person slept there, and not being able to ask them anything about the night.

The hour passes. Odile works at her slate. Once, she looks up at the place on her own board where a reading should be and finds a shape in it: not a blank. A duration. Something with edges, an entry that says an hour happened and refuses to say what it was, and she looks at it a moment longer than she looks at anything.

---

Vela comes out with her face open and the hour gone.

"I'm sorry about breakfast," she says. "I was going to tell you first. I wanted to be the one who told you."

Aster waits, because that is a good sentence, and because Vela's sentences this afternoon should have reasons under them, and Aster wants to hear the reasons.

Vela doesn't have them.

"I don't know why I put it that way," she says, when Aster asks. "It's the right thing to say. I know it's the right thing to say. I can't tell you how I got to it, and I can't look."

"Read it back."

"I can't. That's what we did." She laughs, once, badly. "I paid for this. I'm the payer. I signed."

So that is the price, exactly: the conclusion, and not one step of the road to it. Anyone can be given a conclusion. Aster stands close to her and feels the hole where the hour used to be, and it is not a wound, it is an absence with a shape, and the shape is Vela's.

---

The licence goes back to the Ledger at the end of the day, and Aster has written her own name on the additional-payers line above the signature, in her own hand, before anyone can stop her.

The Ledger returns it at supper, with one line flagged and a date in three days.

*Third payer: consent cannot be verified. A payer whose price cannot be described before it is paid cannot consent to it. Review scheduled.*

Odile reads it twice and sets it down.

"I could undo it," she says. It is the first time anyone has mentioned the possibility out loud. "It's mine to undo. I built the reading."

"Don't," Vela says.

"Don't," Aster says, at the same time, and then looks at Vela, startled, because she had not known that she would say it.

So nothing is undone. The thirty-one rooms are thirty-one rooms. The board has one line crossed out on the afternoon row. And Aster, going up to bed, counts the windows along the corridor the way she counts everything, five in the wall, all opaque, and now one more, somewhere inland of her friend's forehead, a sixth window with nobody looking through it, not even Vela, not even Odile, and the review coming in three days to decide what to do about a price that has to be paid before it can be named.
