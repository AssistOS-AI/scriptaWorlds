# Canon — Humanity's Megatext

## Fundamental laws
- Prohibition that holds in this world: no one was allowed to solve the problem before they could describe who would pay for the solution
- The world is built from the following speculative operations of the Periodic Table of Ideas:
- World Grammar: a universe where elements combine as a language of design (science fantasy and meta-reality, inversion).
- Self-Referential Table: a universe where the catalog of ideas is itself an idea (science fantasy and meta-reality, loop).

These laws are inviolable. Every important event in the book must be a consequence of them, and exceptions
need a cause already established in canon.

Established consequences of the law:
- Nothing in the Earth-Library is closed, built, lit or solved until its payer column is filled: a signed description of who bears the cost.
- An unpaid column does not stay unpaid: it goes to the audit, and the audit removes the shelf. The Three Removals — Wells, Shelley, Verne — were shelves whose payer columns could not be filled.
- The law does not forbid harm. It only makes harm legible, attributable and billable, which is why whitened shelves are recorded as maintenance costs rather than as damage.
- Consequences are inverted in practice: the reader who loses most from a compression is the reader whose name pays for it (chapter 1).

## Ingredients (from the Periodic Table of Ideas)
- World Grammar: a universe where elements combine as a language of design (science fantasy and meta-reality, inversion).
- Self-Referential Table: a universe where the catalog of ideas is itself an idea (science fantasy and meta-reality, loop).

These are the operations the world is a compound of. Every important event must be a consequence of
at least one of them; a newly invented rule needs its own line here, added by ALA.

At work in chapter 1:
- World Grammar: the Survey treats recurring questions as a combinable language of design — sixty-one thousand arrival titles become one well-made node, and the exception field is a permitted grammar for arguing with the map.
- Self-Referential Table: the Survey has begun to grow a peak over its own exception, and the sector is lit by the hunger of one of the readers it compresses.

## World
- The Earth-Library is a shelved archive of four hundred kilometres, and its speculative sector is the part kept after three Removals — the Wells bench, the Shelley bench and the Verne bench.
- The Survey is the classification instrument of the sector: it projects shelves as relief terrain where every peak is a recurring question asked often enough by enough books.
- The Survey's unit of work is the node. Closing a node compresses a set of titles into one question; closing a node requires the surveyor's signature and the sector's payer signature.
- Surveyor work is spine-work: a surveyor reads the shape the books make rather than the books, which is why surveyors routinely compress shelves they have not opened.
- Compression does not remove books from the shelves. Any ordinary reader can still open a compressed title and read every word where it was.
- Every closed node prints a whitening forecast: shelves affected, and readers affected.
- The Survey accepts an exceptions field, at a permanent penalty to the node's confidence rating, and prints the penalty before confirmation.
- The reading room of the speculative sector is warm, smells of orange peel and old glue, and holds the small bench.
- The small bench is the one shelf the previous surveyor refused to classify: two hundred and seventeen thin books, mostly anonymous, mostly written by people who never travelled and wrote about travelling anyway.
- The relief room is the surveyor's workplace; the Warden of the sector does not enter it past the doorway.
- The payer ledger is kept in a booth by the freight lift, in a single long column marked by hand.

## Recurring characters
- **Kesh** — surveyor in the relief room of the Earth-Library's speculative sector. Wants to keep his seat at the projection table and to keep the sector lit. In chapter 1 he signed the arrival node in Aster's house's name, then filed the small bench as unsorted and unresolved against the Survey's rating. Current state: holds the relief table, carries one deliberate inefficiency in his own quarter, and does not know who signed the column before Aster's house.
- **Aster** — reader in the sector, and the one reader in it with White Hunger. Lives on the unsorted shelves, which is counted as maintenance in the payer ledger. Her house has held the sector's payer column since the Verne Removal, sixty-one years, which is why the lights stay on. Wants one shelf where unfinished questions stay unfinished. Current state: fed by twelve shelves for roughly three months if she is careful, and thinner than she was.
- **Dorel** — ledger clerk in the booth by the freight lift. Has answered the same question from three generations of surveyors. State: has marked the sector's payer page, and has stated that the first line under the name is in no hand she has been trained on.
- **The Warden of the sector** — sets the node quotas, speaks the law aloud to make it someone else's idea, and schedules the audits. State: eleven nodes due, an audit in nine days.
- **The Survey** — the instrument itself, addressed as a party: it answers, it penalises, it confirms, and it grows terrain nobody asked for.

## Timeline
- The Three Removals (before the present) — the Wells/ Shelley/ Verne shelves were each one unfilled payer column away from being carried out of the sector in crates.
- Sixty-one years before chapter 1 — Aster's house took over the sector's payer column, at the Verne Removal.

- Chapter 1, same shift — Kesh signs the arrival node sixty-one thousand titles wide, files the small bench's two hundred and seventeen books as unresolved, and accepts the Survey's rating penalty.
- Chapter 1, third day after — Aster finds the Shelley bench whitened; twenty-four hours later the small bench is still black on the page.
- Nine days after chapter 1 (pending) — the sector audit.

## Stable facts
- The sector's payer column has stood in Aster's house since the Verne Removal; her reading of the unsorted shelves is the maintenance that proves the sector is wanted.
- White Hunger is fed by unclassified text only: a page under a closed node becomes white, tasteless and unfilling for a reader who has it. Ordinary readers are unaffected by compression.
- The whitening after a node closes is gradual, over weeks, and raises no alarm, because it is billed as maintenance rather than recorded as damage.
- A surveyor has no power to refuse a node's cost, only to record it: the exception field is the single instrument a surveyor holds.
- Signatures in the payer column are made in ink by hand, and old lines in it can become unreadable through ink laid over ink.
- The Earth-Library's removals are administrative acts, not accidents: a shelf with an unfilled column is carried out in crates.

## Mysteries with a fixed cause
- Why pages go white for Aster — White Hunger is an appetite for undecided text; compression turns a book from unfinished business into an example, and an example holds no food for her. Hints given so far: the whitening forecast names exactly one affected reader in the sector, and Aster says she would have had to "eat white for a year" if Kesh had signed the node knowing whose hunger paid for it.
- Who signed the sector's payer column before Aster's house — fixed cause: the first line under the name was written by the surveyor who filed the small bench and refused to classify it, and the hand is the old surveyor's own invention, which is why no trained clerk can read it. Hints given so far: Dorel has the page marked, says the first line is not in any hand she was trained on, and says "I do not know yet" in the voice of a due date.
- Why the surveyor refused the small bench all those years ago — fixed cause: that surveyor had White Hunger, and the bench was the shelf they were paid to keep open. Hints given so far: the previous surveyor's refusal is mentioned once by Kesh as a fact of the bench's history, with no reason attached.
- What the unnamed peak over the exception is — fixed cause: the Survey, fed an exception it cannot classify, has begun asking the question of its own exception, and the peak is the shape of that question before it has a name. Hints given so far: the bump appears on the Verne side at the end of chapter 1, low and unnamed, with no question attached.
