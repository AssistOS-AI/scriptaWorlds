# The Payer Column

Four hours into the shift, the sector stopped being four hundred kilometres of shelving and became weather.

Kesh rolled his sleeves and let the projection come up. Ridge after ridge of it, white on grey, every peak a question people had asked often enough to leave a mark on the collection. *Is there somewhere else to live, and how do I get there.* *Is this body mine to change.* *Which year would I rather have been born in.* *Who decides who counts.*

Millions of books, pressed down into a landscape you could walk across in an afternoon.

He put his palm flat on the table and the relief answered him the way it always did, politely, with distance.

"Eleven nodes due," said the Warden from the doorway. He never came further in than the doorway. "The audit is in nine days. If the arrival node is still open when they walk through, this room gets reassigned to storage."

"It eats sixty-one thousand titles."

"It compresses sixty-one thousand titles. There is a difference, and the difference is my budget." The Warden tapped the frame. "Fill the columns."

Kesh knew the rule as well as he knew his own hands, but the Warden said it anyway, the way people say the law when they want it to be somebody else's idea.

"Nothing closes until the payer column is filled. You may solve anything you like, Kesh, as long as you can describe who pays for the solving."

Then he was gone, and the doorway was a doorway again.

---

The arrival node covered three benches: the one kept after Wells, the one kept after Shelley, the one kept after Verne. Sixty-one thousand titles that all asked the same thing in different weather — a coast you have not seen, a door in a wall, a sky with two moons and a road out of town. Kesh had spent four months reading their spines without opening them. That was the work. You did not read the books; you read the shape the books made.

He laid the node over the terrain and watched the ridgeline smooth itself. Sixty-one thousand peaks, one question. The Survey ticked, pleased with him. His quarter cleared in one motion. He would keep the room.

Then the lower panel, the one nobody looked at unless they had to, printed the whitening forecast.

**Shelves affected by compression: 12. Affected readers: 1.**

There was only ever one.

The Earth-Library had kept its speculative sector through the Three Removals because shelves stay lit as long as somebody can be described paying for them. That was the law and the law was not cruel; it was only exact. Books did not vanish when a node closed. They stayed on the shelves, warm and fat and full of ink, and any ordinary reader could still open them and find every word where it had been.

But a book under a closed node stopped being unfinished business. It became an example. And for a person with White Hunger — for the one person in the sector who fed on what had not yet been decided — the pages went white, and the white had no taste, and there was no eating it.

Kesh had watched Aster read the same paragraph four times in a week to get one meal out of it.

---

The reading room was warm and smelled of orange peel and old glue. Aster had the small bench, the one the last surveyor had refused to touch: two hundred and seventeen books, mostly thin, mostly written by people who never went anywhere and wrote about going anyway.

She was halfway through one of them, her finger under the line.

"You are early," she said.

"I'm not early. I'm escaping."

"Same thing, from your side of the door." She peeled an orange with her thumb and did not look up. "You have the arrival node on the table."

"On the table and nearly closed."

"Mm." She ate a segment. "Leave me the small bench."

Kesh waited for the rest of it. There usually was no rest of it; she asked for small things in a small voice, and then went back to the page, and it was on you to decide what her asking had cost her.

"Sixty-one thousand is most of the sector," she said. "Twelve shelves. I can live on twelve shelves if I eat quickly. But that bench —" she turned the page — "that bench is the only shelf where somebody wrote down a question without already knowing how it comes out. I eat there every day."

"It's two hundred and seventeen books."

"It's two hundred and seventeen questions nobody has finished answering. That is why I am fat."

She was not fat. She was thin in a way that had stopped being changeable some years ago, the way a bench is thin, the way a shelf is. Kesh did not say any of that. He took a segment of her orange instead, and she let him, and for a minute the reading room was just a reading room.

"I'll see," he said.

"Do not see. Say yes or say no."

"Yes."

She nodded once and went back to her page, and that was the whole negotiation, and it had lasted less than a minute, and it was the largest thing he did that day.

---

To close the node he needed two signatures. His own, under the compression, and the sector's payer, under the shelf-lights.

Dorel kept the ledger in a booth by the freight lift and had the patience of a woman who had been asked the same question by three generations of surveyors.

"The sector's payer column," Kesh said. "For the arrival node."

Dorel turned the book around without hesitation. She had it marked. The column ran the length of the page and the page was old, and the name at the top had been written over so many times that the ink had gone soft and shiny, like a road.

"Aster's house," she said. "Since the Verne Removal. Sixty-one years."

Kesh read it twice. "Aster pays for the lights."

"Aster's house pays for the lights. One reader, one appetite, one column. She reads the unsorted shelves and the ledger counts that as maintenance, because it is. Somebody has to prove the sector is wanted." Dorel capped her pen. "You did not know."

"I did not know."

"Most surveyors know by their second node." She said it without judgment. "There is a first line under the name. Nobody can read it. It is not in any hand I have been trained on."

Kesh leaned in. Under the soft shiny road of the name, one line, older than the rest, written small and deliberately.

"Whose?"

"I do not know yet," Dorel said, in the voice of a person who means a due date and not a refusal.

---

There were two honest roads out of the booth.

Sign the node in Aster's name and the lights stay on, the room stays his, sixty-one thousand books become one well-made question, and twelve shelves go white under Aster's hands over the following weeks — slowly, the way a bruise comes up.

Or leave the node open, and the column for the arrival shelves has no payer, and an unpaid column does not sit quietly in a book. It goes to the audit, and the audit does what it did three times before, when the Wells bench, the Shelley bench and the Verne bench were each one signature away from being carried out of the sector in crates.

Kesh stood in the freight lift for a while with his hand on the rail, going up.

Then he signed the node. Aster's house, in the column, in a line of his own ink under sixty-one years of hers.

And in the exceptions field, in the small box at the bottom of the form that the Survey provides for surveyors who want to argue with it, he wrote two hundred and seventeen numbers and one sentence.

**Small bench, Verne side. Unsorted, unresolved, exempt from compression. Cost to the Survey: permanent.**

The Survey disliked it and said so: **Exception reduces node confidence. Rating penalised. Confirm.**

Confirmed.

---

The node closed at the fourth hour, quietly, the way work does.

Twelve shelves went pale between one shift and the next, and no alarm sounded anywhere, because a whitened page is not an emergency. It is a bill.

Aster noticed on the third day. She had a book from the Shelley bench open in front of her and she turned a page, then turned it back, then put two fingers on the paper as if checking whether a table was level.

"Ah," she said.

"I filed the exception."

"I know. It came up in the projection." She closed the Shelley book and set it down carefully, the way you set down something you will not be picking up again. "Twelve shelves. That is three months of eating, if I'm careful, and I am never careful."

"You could have told me it was your house paying. You could have told me the lights were your hunger."

"And then what?" She got up and went to the small bench and pulled a thin book off it, one of hers, the one with the finger-mark on the spine. "Then you would have signed it looking at me the whole time, and you would have got it wrong, and I would have had to eat white for a year. This way you signed it and then you carved me something out. I like the order better."

She opened the thin book. The print was still black. She read two lines and her shoulders came down an inch, and Kesh understood that he had bought her exactly what she had asked for and nothing more, and that this was, in the ledger of her appetite, a large purchase.

They ate bread and salt and the rest of the orange. She read; he watched the light on the page. Nobody said anything about the column, or the lights, or the sixty-one years, and the room stayed open around them like a hand.

---

Before he went home he put the projection up one last time, out of habit, to check the shift's work.

The ridgeline was smooth where the node had closed. Twelve shelves slept under it.

But on the Verne side, over the small bench, something had grown that had not been there in the morning: a low unnamed peak, barely a bump, right where the exception had been written.

The Survey had no question for it yet. It had only the shape of one — the shape of a place two hundred and seventeen people had asked about without finishing, and one surveyor had been told, by hand, in ink, to leave alone.
