Reference

Wiki

This is the canonical terminology page of scriptaWorlds. Every project-specific term, file, role and mechanism used in the documentation has one entry here with the anchor other pages link to. Each entry states what the term means, what it is for, who or what uses it, how it behaves, and where its limits are.

The book and its concepts

Universe

A universe is one persistent science-fiction book together with everything that belongs to it: a folder under universes/, the law that defines it, its canon, its chapters, its reading history, its version archive and its editions. It is created from a start template of the library, from a compound of ingredients chosen in the table of ideas, or from a client's own law, premise and language, and it is identified by a slug derived from the title that existed at creation. Readers select a universe from the header of the reader interface; the server lists them sorted by the time of the last change, so the book that was just continued appears first. A universe is open or closed, and closing it prevents new chapters without deleting anything.

Its title is optional at creation. A reader starting from a template sends no title, so the universe starts with a provisional name and the flag autoTitle: true, and the first successful chapter names it: the agent writes one short name to universe-title.txt and the server adopts it, clears the flag and keeps the descriptive line as the summary shown under the title. A universe created by an integration that sends a title is named immediately, and a title states what is unique in that world rather than decorating it, because the title is how a reader chooses between books.

Fundamental law

The fundamental law is the rule, stated at the metaphysical level, that defines a universe: how the world exists, what sustains life, and which constraints are absolute. It is the identity of the universe, not its decoration, and the product refuses to create a universe without one: without a law and without an ingredient the creation is rejected with the error code BAD_LAW, and a law shorter than 24 characters is rejected the same way. A law written by the client comes first and the lines of the chosen ingredients follow it, which is what composeLaw does with a compound. The law is stored in the universe metadata, repeated in the charter, placed as the first section of the canon, and included in every prompt sent to the agent with the instruction that no chapter may break it and that a second fundamental law may only be introduced through a cause already established in the canon. Civilizations, technologies, religions and conflicts are consequences of the law; a character, a civilization or a single idea is not a substitute for it.

Universe identifier

The universe identifier is the folder name and the key used in every route and file name, and it must match the pattern [a-z0-9][a-z0-9-]{2,63}. It is derived from the title by a slug function that removes diacritics, lowercases the text, collapses everything else into hyphens and truncates the result, falling back to univers when nothing usable remains. When the derived identifier is already taken, the server appends a counter, and it answers ID_TAKEN when it cannot find a free one. Two readers can therefore choose the same title without colliding, and the identifier never changes afterwards.

Short name

A short name is the title ALA gives a book it has to name: the prompt asks for four or five words like a book title the reader will see in the header, and the server accepts anything from two to seven words up to sixty characters, so a longer descriptive line is refused and the naming duty stays in the prompt of the following turns. It is written as the first non-empty line of universe-title.txt and adopted after the turn, which clears autoTitle and keeps the earlier descriptive line as summary, shown as the discreet line under the title in the reader interface. universe-title.txt is a hand-off file rather than a permanent part of the folder: the server removes it once it has adopted the name, so a universe never keeps a stale line that contradicts its title. A universe whose displayed title is still a whole sentence — more than seven words — is renamed the same way at its next chapter.

ALA

ALA is the narrative agent of a universe: the role the prompt assigns to the coding agent, and the voice a reader meets in the interface. In fiction ALA is close to a god of its world; as a process it is the agent that plans an episode, writes it and commits its consequences to the canonical files. ALA speaks to the reader in the offer that follows every chapter and in the invitation of the start screen, and it is also ALA that names a book that was created without a title, by writing a short name for the world it has just written. It is not a separate service: ALA is what the agent process is asked to be during a turn, and every one of its chapters is required to be a consequence of the universe's law.

Chapter

A chapter, also called an episode, is one reading unit of a book: a file named chapters/NNNN-slug.md whose first line is the title and whose body is narrative text in the language of the book. A chapter carries one dramatic question, is expected to fall inside the configured word band, closes at least one old promise after the first episode, and ends with at most one major hook. Its prose uses a small Markdown subset, headings, paragraphs, blockquotes, scene separators and emphasis, because that is what both the browser renderer and the print renderer accept. The reading order of a book is the numeric order of chapter numbers, and a chapter is always produced by exactly one turn, which the chapter exposes as its turn number.

Offer

The offer is ALA's direct address to the reader at the end of a chapter: a file chapters/NNNN-offer.json holding a teaser of two or three sentences and two or three concrete decisions, each with a short label and the exact prompt that can be sent as the next request. It exists so that continuing a book does not require composing a prompt: the reader presses a decision and the agent receives its prompt verbatim, or copies the prompt into the input field to change it first. The server normalizes the file before publishing it, bounding the teaser, the labels and the prompts and dropping unusable options, and exposes the same object on the chapter list and on a single chapter read. A missing or unusable offer is recorded as a warning on the turn rather than failing it, and the interface then falls back to a continuation idea.

Chapter version

A chapter version is an earlier text of a chapter that has been rewritten, stored as chapters/.history/NNNN-vK.md and numbered by counting the entries already archived for that chapter. Archiving happens before a rewrite overwrites a chapter and also when later chapters are dropped by a confirmed rewrite, which makes the archive append-only: the text of every superseded version can be recovered, and a chapter read reports how many versions exist so the interface can mark it as rewritten. The archive is not a draft area; it is history, and nothing in the product edits it.

Charter

The charter is the file charter.md that the server writes when a universe is created and that a human or client may edit afterwards. It holds the language of the fiction, the fundamental law as an inviolable section, and the permanent narrative rules of that universe: one dramatic question per episode, limits on new characters, names and concepts, the requirement that every cosmological idea be lived by a character with a concrete want, honesty and agency rules, and the prohibition of retroactive canon changes without an explicit fork. The agent reads the charter before writing and treats it as a constraint with priority over its own preferences, which is what lets an operator tune a single universe without touching the skill that all universes share.

Canon

The canon is the file canon.md, the state of the world the agent maintains across chapters. Its sections are the fundamental laws, the ingredients the universe is composed from, the world, the recurring characters, the timeline, the stable facts, and the mysteries with a fixed cause; text values are written in the language of the book while section names stay English. The agent adds short verifiable facts and only removes an old fact when the episode contradicts it, and it must say why. The server writes the canon once, when the universe is created, and overwrites it from a snapshot when a turn has to be rolled back or a chapter is rewritten; between those moments it only reads a bounded summary for the universe detail, and it never edits the narrative facts the agent recorded.

Threads

The thread list is the file threads.json, the bookkeeping of everything the story has left open: open and closed threads, promises made to the reader, and answers that were deferred. An entry carries an identifier, a kind of promise, mystery, decision or question, its text, the chapter that created it, the chapter by which it should pay off, and a status of open, deferred, closed or abandoned, with the closing chapter and the resolution for a closed entry. The agent is required to record a deferred revelation here with its due chapter, and the server reads the file to derive continuation ideas and to expose the thread summary of a universe. The file is part of the narrative contract: the agent maintains its content, and the server writes it only when the universe is created and when it restores a snapshot.

Atlas

The atlas is the file atlas.json, the record of which thematic axes an episode touched. It keeps a version marker and an array of axes, each with an English identifier and a name and an array of nodes; a node has an identifier, a label, the chapters that touched it, and a state of mentioned, dramatized, decision or recontextualized. The twelve axes are mind and identity, life and death, time and causality, artificial autonomy, civilization and power, abundance and scarcity, alien alterity, reality and simulation, body and evolution, knowledge and truth, cosmic scale, and culture and meaning. The atlas is what keeps a long book from retelling the same idea: the creative palette in the narrative skill lists the axes so the agent can choose one that has not been dramatized yet, and the validator warns when a chapter touched no node at all.

Episode plan

The episode plan is the file drafts/NNNN-plan.md that the agent writes before the prose of a chapter. It holds fixed keys, one per line: the dramatic question, the anchor character and what that character wants, the primary speculative idea, the human need, the opening hook, four to six causal beats, the decision where the reader has agency, the local consequence visible in the same episode, a longer horizon, the payoff, at most one return hook, the new entities, and at most one new deferred answer with its due chapter. The plan is not canonical: the chapter is the artifact of record, and the plan exists so that the invariants can be checked before the text is written and so that the reader can see how an episode was built.

Template catalog

The template catalog is the list of proposed universes behind templates/universes.json, which lets a client start a book from a prepared proposal without inventing a law. Each entry has an identifier, tags, and a localized descriptive title, law and set of openings; an opening is a concrete first move that names who is in the scene, which problem they face and what is at stake, and each entry carries three or four of them so that a reader can choose the start of the story instead of writing it. The server serves the list through the templates route, resolving each localized value in the order requested language, English, Romanian, and dropping any entry that lacks an identifier, a title or a law.

The catalog is assembled from a main file plus optional part files under templates/parts/, merged by identifier with the first occurrence winning, so it can be split into thematic files without changing the reader. Its size is not part of any interface: the route reports a count and clients read that count. This catalog is no longer the way into the product: the reader interface starts from the library of start templates and from the table of ideas, and it reads neither this route nor these entries, which stay available to API clients and keep their content unchanged. Creating a universe from a proposal still copies the chosen title, law and opening into the new book and keeps no link back to the template, so editing the catalog never changes an existing universe.

Exploration

Exploration is the product's name for what a reader does with a universe: entering a possible world defined by its law, reading what happens in it, and deciding what should happen next. The exploration surface is what the interface shows when no universe is selected: the start screen, which opens with one short paragraph under an information glyph — what a template is, and that nothing starts before Send — then three tabs. Custom holds one large field for a world the reader writes themselves and one Start control: the text is the specification of the new universe — what exists, what is impossible, who is in it, what is already wrong — and pressing Start creates that universe with the text as its fundamental law and begins its first chapter from the same text, which is the same composition the empty template performs. A text shorter than a sentence is refused in the panel, and the answer of the server appears under the same field. Library lists the start templates of the library, each row with its title, sector, genealogy, one-line summary and cell symbols, and choosing one loads the template: it is read in full next to the list, with the indications the book gives for that night and the night itself in a fold, and its Use control puts the request of the template into the request field of the composer, turning into In use while the template holds that field. Ingredients shows the table of ideas as a grid of twelve families by fifteen operators with a search box, a legend of the operators, at most six choices, a control that clears them and a fold for proposing an element the table does not contain; a cell, an operator or a family opens the Details dialog, which shows what stands behind it — for a cell the idea, what its family changes, where it is seen and the nights that already use it — and can insert the cell into the compound. Only Custom creates on its own; Library and Ingredients put text in the field below, and no tab holds a title field, a premise field or a language selector.

The composer field is the input of the interface for every world that is already open, and the language of the new book is chosen in the header selector, which the browser remembers. The two composing tabs build one text rather than excluding each other: a template that was chosen stays chosen while the reader browses the table, the ingredients stay chosen while the reader browses the library, and the field holds the request of the template followed by an Operations added by me: block in which each chosen operation appears as its ingredient sentence, never as the two-letter address the table uses for it. Pressing Send creates the universe from both — POST /api/universes carries library and elements together, and the server adds the chosen ingredients on top of the cells of the template, without duplicates and at most six in all — and starts its first chapter from the edited text in the same request, so an exploration begins with a chapter rather than with an empty book, and the new book is named by that chapter with a short name. Text alone composes a world too: without a template and without an ingredient, the request text becomes the law of the new universe, which is exactly what Custom and the empty template send. The vocabulary is part of the design: the interface talks about exploring universes rather than about writing files or administering a project.

The Table and the library

Periodic Table of Speculative Ideas

The Periodic Table of Speculative Ideas is the design instrument of the project: twelve families across fifteen operators, one hundred and eighty operations in all, kept in data/periodic-table.json after being read out of vision/periodic_table.pdf. A cell carries a systematic identifier from 01.01 to 12.15, a systematic symbol built from the letter of its family and the letter of its operator — CA is COSMOS with ALTERATION — the mnemonic symbol the book prints for it, a name, a one-line gist, a prompt line, a literary landmark and the prose the book devotes to it. It exists because a universe is not a prompt but a compound of a few operations, and a table makes the choice of those operations visible and repeatable. src/periodic.mjs reads the table, GET /api/table serves the grid — the twelve families with the cells that belong to them, the fifteen operators and one line per cell — GET /api/table/cells/:symbol serves everything the book says about one cell, resolveElements resolves a reader's selection and composeLaw composes the law from it.

Design instrument

Design instrument is the name the project gives to the printed book vision/periodic_table.pdf and to the artifact the product reads out of it. It is a design instrument because it is consulted before anything is written: npm run table reads the data file out of the book and its --check mode compares the file with the book without writing, and npm run library turns the nights of the same book into the seventy-four start templates of the library. It is never a text the reader has to study: the interface shows the table as a grid of named operations with a legend, and the reader picks from it while the request text composes itself in the field.

Family

A family is one of the twelve rows of the table: COSMOS, SPACE, TIME, LIFE, MIND, BODY, INFORMATION, SOCIETY, ARTIFACT, CONTACT, FANTASY and META-REALITY. Every family has its own letter, used in the systematic symbol of an operation, and each operation belongs to exactly one family, which is the field of reality it acts on. A family is not a genre label: it says what kind of thing is changed — matter and scale, distance, order in time, the living, the mental, the bodily, information, the social, the made, the encountered, the impossible, and the world that describes itself.

Operator

An operator is one of the fifteen columns of the table, the mechanism an operation applies: ALTERATION, SUBSTITUTION, EXTENSION, RATE, BOUNDARY, DISTRIBUTION, COPYING, COUPLING, EXTERNALIZATION, INVERSION, LOOP, GENESIS, SCARCITY SHIFT, REFLEXIVITY and OUTFINITE. Each operator has a single letter used in systematic symbols and one sentence saying what it does, for example that alteration changes the value or form of an existing rule without changing the kind of thing it acts on. The interface shows the operators as the columns of the grid and as a legend next to it, because the same mechanism read in another family is another idea.

Cell

A cell is one square of the table — the intersection of a family and an operator, and therefore one speculative operation with a name, an identifier and a symbol. There are exactly one hundred and eighty of them, and npm run check asserts that count, asserts that every systematic symbol is the letter of its family followed by the letter of its operator, and opens a cell to confirm that it offers the idea, the sentence about what that family changes and the worlds that already use it, instead of the book's repeated skeleton. A cell is what a reader can choose as an ingredient, and it is what a start template names when it says which operations are already at work in its world. Every cell carries a prompt line, one sentence written for the request field — the interface can drop it in unchanged — and an ingredient sentence that names the cell and says what it does, in the form Neutron Life: a universe where organisms live in degenerate matter (life and evolution, extension). The ingredient sentence is what every text written for a person carries: the composed law, the Ingredients section of the canon, the AGENTS.md inside the universe folder, the first-chapter request and the chapter and rewrite prompts. The two-letter symbol is only an address — it identifies the cell inside the table, inside a template and inside universe.json, and it never appears in that prose; npm run check asserts exactly that for the ingredient lines, the composed law and the AGENTS.md, and it shows the symbol in the grid and in the library list only as the address of an operation.

Opening a cell — from the grid, or through GET /api/table/cells/:symbol — shows what that operation offers a reader: the idea in one line (the essence) with the longer prompt under it, what its family changes (familyNote), the operator with the sentence saying what it does, the work it is Seen in, and the worlds that already use it — at most three, with the heading counting them — each with its title, sector, the situation it starts in, the problem already under way there, and a control that opens that template in the Library tab. The raw prose the book devotes to a cell is deliberately not served: it repeats a skeleton across all one hundred and eighty entries, so the payload distils it into those fields and the grid stays small while the reader still sees what the cell is for and where it has already been used.

Ingredient

An ingredient is one operation a reader chooses when a universe is composed. It is stored in universe.json as an entry of elements with its symbol, name, family, operator and gist, and it is either a cell of the table or an element the reader proposes, which is marked custom: true and carries a name and, optionally, a family, an operator and a note. At most six ingredients compose one universe. They are resolved by src/periodic.mjs before anything is written, listed in canon.md under Ingredients, named in the AGENTS.md of the universe and repeated in every chapter and rewrite prompt, so the agent writes from the recipe and not from a summary of it. The law, the canon, the AGENTS.md of the universe and the agent prompts carry them as sentences rather than as addresses — a cell contributes its ingredient sentence, a proposed element its name and note — so no two-letter symbol appears in the composed law, in the canon, in the folder instructions or in a prompt. A symbol the table does not contain is refused with UNKNOWN_ELEMENT, an unknown family with BAD_ELEMENT_FAMILY, an unknown operator with BAD_ELEMENT_OPERATOR and a malformed list with BAD_ELEMENTS.

Compound

A compound is a universe understood as the combination of the operations it was composed from: at most six ingredients, whose lines composeLaw appends to the fundamental law. A law the client wrote comes first and the ingredient lines follow it, and a creation with neither a law nor an ingredient is refused with BAD_LAW. The word matters because it says what the product does: it does not generate a prompt, it composes a world out of a small number of chosen operations whose consequences the agent must then respect in every chapter.

Library

The library is the folder library/ that holds the worlds a reader can start from: library/index.json with one line per template — slug, kind, number, title, sector, genealogy, cell symbols and summary — and library/<slug>/template.json with the template itself, read only when a template is chosen. The index also carries cellScenes, the reverse index of the table: for a symbol, up to three start templates that already work that cell, each with its title, sector, situation and story. It holds the empty template first and then the seventy-four nights of the book, seventy-five entries in all. It is rebuilt from the printed table by npm run library, which requires pdftotext and writes the index and every template folder again; npm run library -- --check compares the index with the folders and with the book and writes nothing. GET /api/library serves the index and GET /api/library/:slug serves one template, which is why the start screen lists seventy-five worlds without reading seventy-five folders. The library is deliberately separate from the older template catalog, which the interface no longer reads and which is kept unchanged for API clients.

Start template

A start template is one world a reader can begin from: a night of the book reduced to what a universe needs before its first chapter — a title, a sector, a genealogy, the cells that are active in it, the prohibition that holds there, the situation the story starts in, one central problem, a summary of that problem, a request text, the indications as a labelled list and the night as it is printed. The request adds nothing generic to what the book says and never announces a course: it positions the world for the reader — A world in the AUTHORS-HALL sector, kept after Shelley; Stapledon; Borges. — and then states what the world is built from and where the story arrives. The empty template reads A new world, with nothing fixed yet: this first request becomes its first law. in the same field, and the reader edits either of them before sending. Choosing a template in the Library tab puts that text into the request field and shows the rest next to the list; nothing exists until the reader sends it. The server then builds the universe from the template with creationFromTemplate in src/library.mjs: the law from the prohibition, the premise from the situation and the story, the cells as ingredients — with the operations the reader added on top, without duplicates and at most six — and no title, because ALA names the book after the first chapter with a short name. A slug the library does not contain is refused with UNKNOWN_TEMPLATE and a malformed slug with BAD_TEMPLATE.

Indications

Indications are what the book states about a night before any story is written, carried by a start template as a labelled list: Sector, Genealogy, Prohibition when the night states one, Active cells, Situation, Central tension and Seen in. In a template file the field is indications, an array of {label, text} pairs. They exist so that a reader can see what a world is built from without reading the night itself: the Library tab shows them next to the request of the chosen template, and the request repeats the same material as prose written for the request field. The hand-written empty template carries no indications, because no night stands behind it.

Empty template

The empty template is the first entry of the library, marked kind: "empty", the one template that is written by hand instead of being read from the book. It carries no prohibition and no cells, because it is the world in which no law is fixed yet, and the first request the reader sends becomes its first law. It exists so that a reader who wants no prepared world still starts from the same field as everyone else, with nothing decided in advance.

Persistence, execution and failure

Turn

A turn is one client request together with the agent execution it caused, and it is the unit of work, of queueing and of history in the product. Every turn has a number inside its universe, a kind that says whether it writes a chapter or an edition, a status, and a durable record on disk. A chapter turn produces one chapter and its offer; a rewrite turn produces a new version of one existing chapter; an export turn produces the printed files. A turn is created before any work starts, which is what makes the product survive a restart, and its number is separate from the chapter number so that rewriting a chapter does not erase the record of what happened before.

Turn record

A turn record is the file turns/NNNN.json that describes one turn: its number, kind, status, timestamps and duration, the request it came from, the model that ran it, the requested format, the chapter it produced or the exports it produced, the agent's final answer, the composed log of the tools it used and the text it wrote, any warnings, the normalized offer, and the error message when it failed. Requests read it as a summary in the universe detail, the full record of one turn is read through GET /api/universes/:id/turns/:number, and the console of a run is rendered from the turn journal beside it through GET /api/universes/:id/turns/:number/console, live while the turn runs and afterwards. Turn records live as long as the universe folder does; they are never rewritten except to advance the status of the turn they describe.

Import

An import is a book the team did not write entering the same store as a written one. The upload is a PDF or a DOCX, it is stored outside universes/ beside the assessment workspace, its text is extracted without a model, and the universe it becomes is written by import turns under scripta-import, so the book is structurally identical to a written one and can afterwards be reviewed, measured, rewritten chapter by chapter and printed like any other. It reaches the store through POST /api/imports (the body is the file, its name in the X-Filename header, optionally X-format: pdf|docx), GET /api/imports, GET /api/imports/:id, POST /api/imports/:id/extract and POST /api/imports/:id/universe, which creates the universe or continues the import. The upload is bounded while it streams in: a body larger than IMPORT_MAX_BYTES — 64 MiB unless the environment says otherwise — is refused during the transfer with 413 IMPORT_TOO_LARGE (contracts §8.6).

Extraction

An extraction is the host's reading of an uploaded book: a book-import.v1 document written beside the upload as extracted.json, with book.md as its readable twin and a copy staged at .agents/import/ inside the universe so the import turn reads its only source of prose from its own folder. It carries the source (file name, format, sha256, byte count and page count), the detected metadata (title, author, language) and the chapters, each with a number, a title, a word count and its text with paragraphs separated by blank lines and headings kept out of it, plus the warnings that say what the host could not read. It is produced without a model and deterministically — the same file always yields the same text — by reading a DOCX through its own ZIP central directory and inflating only the parts the text needs with node:zlib, or by inflating the content streams of a PDF and reading its text operators; a PDF with no text layer is refused with IMPORT_NO_TEXT rather than guessed at. A book whose boundaries the file itself shows arrives segmented, by a DOCX heading style or a short standalone heading line in a PDF; one whose boundaries cannot be detected arrives as a single chapter with a warning that says so (contracts §8.6).

Import turn

An import turn is one queued turn of kind import in which scripta-import copies a bounded window of the extraction into the store: the chapter files of that window, the state files canon.md, threads.json and atlas.json, and the import record that says what was imported. Its bound belongs to the skill, not to the server: one turn covers at most three extraction chapters and at most 6,000 words of their prose, whichever comes first, and always at least one chapter, so a single long chapter travels alone; a turn above that bound is reported as the warning TURN_OVERSIZE and does not refuse the import. The host plans the window with the same function the skill uses and verifies the result afterwards by running the skill's validator over the range the turn wrote, so a turn whose chapters are missing fails with NO_CHAPTER and one the validator refuses fails with the validator's own code. The text of an imported book reaches the store only through such a turn.

Import record

The import record is drafts/import-progress.json inside the universe, written by the import turn and authoritative over the chapters on disk: a chapter file does not carry the number of the extraction chapter it came from, so the store alone cannot say what has been imported. It is a book-import.v1 document that marks the book as an import, names the upload it came from by import_id and source.sha256, lists the chapters it wrote with their file and source chapter, the chapters it skipped with a reason, the turns it ran, the language block that compares the book's language with the state's and the universe's, and the segmentation method it used. Its continuation pointer is next_source_chapter, the extraction chapter the next turn starts at, and complete is true exactly when that pointer is null; the host reads the pointer to plan the next window. A record that names another upload is refused with INVALID_IMPORT rather than repaired by guessing, and a record whose import_id or source does not match the extraction is a defect rather than a merge (contracts §8.6).

State snapshot

A state snapshot is the full copy of the accepted book — canon.md, threads.json, atlas.json and every accepted chapter plus its offer — that the server takes before every turn starts, into a fresh staging directory that becomes turns/NNNN.prev/ only after each copied file has been verified against the manifest written beside it. A snapshot is therefore complete or absent, never half-copied, and its manifest names the inventory, the role of every file and the content identity of the version it protects. It is deliberately kept after a successful turn, because it is exactly the book as it was before that chapter existed: it is what makes a later rewrite of that chapter possible and what restores the last accepted book when a turn fails or is interrupted. A restore puts back the bytes and the membership of the accepted book — a chapter the agent invented, a renamed target or an extra plan disappears with it — and it is repeatable, so the snapshot is not consumed; the version archive and the produced editions are never touched. The price of rewritable history is therefore that one snapshot folder per chapter remains on disk, and the benefit is that no component ever has to guess what the canon looked like earlier.

Queue

The queue is the FIFO list of turns waiting for a universe, and it exists so that a reader never has to wait for a previous request to finish before sending the next one. A request is appended even when the universe is already writing, is answered immediately with a job that reports its position, and starts in turn order; different universes run their turns in parallel, and each universe runs at most one turn at a time. The queue is persisted rather than kept in memory: the turn record is written with the status queued before the request is acknowledged, so a restart resumes the waiting turns in the same order with the same requests. There is no implicit ceiling on simultaneous turns across the server; the concurrency limit is optional.

Job

A job is the live handle on a turn while the server process runs: an identifier, the universe, the kind, the requested format, the status, the timestamps, the turn number, the request text, the position in its universe queue and, at the end, the result or the error. A new turn receives a random job identifier and a retried turn reuses an identifier derived from its universe and turn number. Jobs exist only in memory, so a job identifier from an earlier process is no longer found, and the durable description of the same work is the turn record. The job snapshot is what a client receives when it submits a request, what the jobs route returns, and what the event stream repeats while the work progresses.

Store lock

The store lock is the file universes/.server.lock that a server takes, with acquireStoreLock in src/lock.mjs, before it touches anything else in the store, and releases when it stops. It exists because two servers on one store corrupt real work: each of them would treat the turns found on disk as its own and mark the running turns of the other as interrupted, and both would resume the same queue. The file holds the process identifier and the start time of the owner; a second instance refuses to start while that process is alive, printing that another server already owns the folder, and takes the lock over when the process is gone. Only node src/server.mjs starts a server and takes the lock: importing the module has no side effects, because the check suite imports it to read the function that maps a creation request body.

Interrupted turn

An interrupted turn is a turn that was running when the server stopped. At the next startup the server converts every record with the status running into interrupted, restores the accepted book from the turn's snapshot — bytes and membership, so the partial chapter and the offer of that turn disappear with everything else the agent left behind — and writes the completed state back to the record with the reason. A turn whose snapshot is missing or unusable is not called interrupted: it becomes recovery_required, and its book refuses further work rather than pretending its accepted state is known. The reader sees it as a failed item with an explanation and a retry action rather than as a chapter that mysteriously changed. Nothing that the interrupted turn had produced survives into the book, which is why a restart can never leave half a chapter behind.

Recovery-required turn

A recovery-required turn is a turn whose accepted state could not be restored, because the snapshot taken before it is missing, incomplete or does not match its manifest. It is the honest answer to a situation the server cannot repair by itself: instead of marking the turn failed and letting the next request write on top of a book whose contents are unknown, the server records recovery_required and then refuses every new turn and every retry of that universe with the code RECOVERY_REQUIRED until a human establishes the accepted version by hand. Retrying such a turn first attempts the restore again and is only accepted when that restore succeeds, at which point the turn returns to interrupted with a reason that states the repair.

Approved direction

An approved direction is a short instruction that a separate design or craft phase produced and that a human accepted, carried into a later writing request as input data. It is not a handbook: the chapter prompt states it under the fundamental law and the accepted canon, so a direction can never override either, and the agent is asked to report which directions it used. The turn record keeps the directions together with the approval record they came from — its path, its hash and the accepted version it was written against — so a chapter can be traced back to the decision that shaped it. A design phase writes its proposal outside the universe, and only an explicit human approval makes any part of it travel this way.

Generic review

A generic review is an assessment that needs nothing but a book: the host builds its own evaluation profile with a resolved scope, runs the deterministic review, and reports the components — with an advisory label instead of a calibration claim. It exists because a review has to be usable before any human study exists; a study can later test whether a particular rubric or aggregate agrees with readers, and only that claim needs study evidence. Optional aggregation runs under the research policy with declared weights, an advisory note and, when the caller states one, an emotional intention; without a stated intention the aggregate is unavailable with its reason and the components stand on their own.

Assessment run

An assessment run is one execution of one separate-phase skill over one frozen packet, recorded outside the universe as run.json. It carries a run identifier that is independent of turn and chapter numbering, the phase and the trigger that started it (requested or arc), the accepted version it reviewed, the scope and profile it used, its status, the files it published and the envelope the skill printed. Two runs are the same assessment when their input fingerprints are equal, and a second request then returns the run that already exists instead of reviewing the same evidence twice. A published run is immutable: its report keeps the bytes and the revision a reader's reaction refers to, and re-evaluating it creates a new run that names the one it re-evaluates. A review a reader no longer wants is deleted for good — the run folder, its frozen packet, its console and its published report together — and the host refuses that while the run is still queued or running and while another record names the report. A run whose version is no longer the accepted one is historical, not current. A run that asked a model for its observations records which mode produced them, which model answered and with which settings, which prompt version and prompt bytes were used, every attempt with its identity, its exit code, its validation errors and the length of the answer, the hashes of the rubric, case and vocabulary material the prompt was built from, the path and byte hash of the accepted document, the evaluator identity the host derived from its own invocation, and whether the frozen packet stayed byte-identical.

Report revision

A report revision is the identity of one published review result: the name, hash and size of every file the run published, hashed together into the run's result_revision. It exists so that a reaction, a finding link or a feedback entry can say which published text it was about. A review that is published is never replaced: re-evaluating it writes a new run with its own revision, and a stored file whose bytes no longer match the revision it was published with is refused when it is read instead of being served as if it were the same report.

Rubric anchors

Rubric anchors are the published meaning of the ratings a review asks for: for every dimension of a judged metric a definition, the evidence question a rating answers, a description of each rating from 0 to 4, and the justified exceptions, together with the same material for the eight literary indicators and a declared default that ranks no quiet scene, static character, closed ending or local cultural setting as a defect by itself. They live in skills/scripta-metrics-report/schema/rubric-anchors.v1.json with a prose twin in references/rubric-anchors.md, and they exist because a rating without a declared scale is a number nobody can interpret or dispute.

Teaching case

A teaching case is one pair of short synthetic passages that differ in exactly one literary feature, published in the metrics skill's case library with its target distinction, its expected evidence at real byte offsets and the alternative readings it accepts. A bounded, language-matched selection of these cases travels with a review request so the evaluator can see what a distinction looks like, while a declared subset stays out of that selection as regression material; no case contains a human judgement, which is why agreement with a real evaluator is recorded separately and starts as unperformed.

Evidence eligibility

Evidence eligibility is the rule that a judgement may rest only on the text the review was asked to read. An evidence item is verified against the frozen bytes and against the reviewed selection: a claim whose evidence lies outside the selection is refused with EVIDENCE_OUT_OF_SCOPE and nothing is written, and a claim whose evidence lies entirely in declared context chapters is demoted to an unavailable result naming its reason instead of being scored. A model cannot count bytes in a file it reads through a paged view, so it may name the line a passage is on and quote it: the host resolves that pair to the exact bytes of the frozen file, refuses a quotation that is not between the lines it names or that occurs there more than once, and records that it resolved the offsets. The rule exists because a rating about unexamined prose looks like a measurement while it is only an assertion.

Reading status

The four-value statement a metrics report publishes about how much of the selection was actually judged: not_evaluated (no judgement was produced), insufficient_evidence (part of the selection was not read), no_supported_issue_found (all of it was read without a supported problem) and problems_recorded. It exists so an empty findings list is never read as a clean book.

Evaluator provenance

The record the evaluator itself declares about the run that produced an annotation document: the prompt, the rubric and case hashes, the resource selection, the model and its settings, the attempt identities and any provider usage it observed. A report carries it verbatim under its own digest and never completes it, so a missing model identity or usage figure stays absent instead of being inferred.

Annotation stage

The annotation stage is the part of a review that asks a model for observations instead of measuring them: the host composes a prompt from what scripta-metrics-report publishes — its annotation vocabulary, its anchored 0..4 rubric with the evidence question of every dimension and the rule that a quiet scene, a static character, a closed ending or a local cultural setting is not a defect by default, the definitions and intended-effect qualifications of its eight indicators, and a bounded selection of real paired teaching cases in the book's language, never one of its declared regression holdout — runs the agent binary with the read-only tool list read,grep,glob in the run folder of the assessment workspace against the frozen packet, and validates the answer before the review sees it — the schema version, the source version it claims, evidence that resolves to the exact bytes of the packet, and the bounds on evidence and segments. A rejected answer gets one bounded repair call whose prompt names the fields that failed, and an answer that still yields no usable document fails the run with 502 ANNOTATION_FAILED. The accepted document is stored as generated/annotations.json beside the run and never inside the packet, whose bytes stay exactly the book the review was asked about, so a review can hand the same observations to a model-free consumer. The stage also keeps the exact prompt of every attempt with its hash, the hashes of the published material the prompt was built from, the model and the settings the evaluator ran with, the identity of each attempt and the byte hash of every artifact it wrote, so a later session can establish what the evaluator saw; the evaluator identity is derived from the host's own invocation rather than from the label the answer carries. The stage is optional: a caller may bring its own annotations instead, and a deployment configured for the deterministic mode calls no model at all.

Applicable rules

Applicable rules are the rules a review measures a book against, declared by the host and frozen into the assessment packet before any evaluator exists, as the artifact of role rules at input/rules/applicable-rules.v1.json. They come from documents the host can verify — the permanent charter of the universe, which travels beside the registry as rules/charter.md, the general rule set scripta-metrics-report publishes at schema/rules.v1.json (registry_version stg-rules.v1), the world laws an approved design.v1 brief of the captured version declares, named brief-<assumption id>, and the directions a human accepted, named direction-<8 hex> — and every one of them is recorded with the path and hash it was read from. A document that was never recorded is listed as missing with its reason and no rule is derived from it, because an imported book has no authoring request and absence is not a violation. The evaluator is asked for an outcome per rule and output pair and never for the rules themselves, so the standard a report states is the host's declaration rather than something a model invented.

Bounded reading

A bounded reading is how a generic review reads a whole book without asking for more than one call can carry: the selection is planned as units — one unit per chapter, or one per byte range of a chapter larger than the per-unit budget, each with the context chapters the caller permitted and, for a range, the rest of its own chapter — and each unit is evaluated on its own against the frozen packet. The plan is written to generated/units/plan.json beside the run before the first evaluator call and names the limits it was planned under, a unit that does not fit the budget is declared unread rather than summarised, and one artifact per unit at generated/units/<unit id>.json lets an interrupted reading resume the units it already paid for. A synthesis then draws the selection's conclusions from those verified observations instead of averaging the per-unit grades, and the reading record states whether the claim covers the whole selection or only the part of it that was read.

Continuity substage

The continuity substage is the scripta-continuity-review skill run by the host itself, as a separate deterministic child process, over the same frozen assessment packet a metrics review was captured from, publishing into continuity/ inside that run's own directory. Its child costs no model call, its result is bound to the captured version and narrowed to the selection the review was asked about: the counts are recomputed from the comparison records that lie in the reviewed chapters, and a comparison that cannot be attributed to any of them is reported as unattributable rather than folded into the rate. What it found reaches the metrics phase as the continuity member of the annotations document, and a substage that refuses the packet, times out or exits non-zero is recorded with its reason while the review still publishes, because continuity is one input among several and never a gate on a literary reading.

Approval

An approval is the recorded human decision on a proposal a separate design or craft phase produced: approved or declined, by whom, at what moment, against which accepted version, and with the concrete directions that were accepted. It is the only bridge between a phase and a later writing request, it lives outside the universe beside the run that produced the proposal, and it is never rewritten — a second decision on the same proposal and version is refused. A declined proposal carries no directions and stays declined.

Arc completion

Arc completion is a recorded event that an arc of a book is finished, declared against one accepted version: it names the arc identifier and the version, is written outside the universe, and is what may start the assessment an evaluation profile implies. It exists because a completed flag inside a design proposal is still a proposal: a plan cannot establish that something happened in the book. The event is deduplicated with the run it starts, so declaring the same arc complete for the same version and profile twice does not review the book twice.

Reader feedback

Reader feedback is what the people who read a book say about it, and it is stored beside the reviews rather than inside the book: it is evidence about one version of the text, it belongs to the readers who gave it, and it has to survive every rewrite and every re-import of the store. Its unit is a target — the accepted version, an arc or one chapter as a reader saw it — and a target is a copy of that text, byte for byte, kept outside universes/ in the assessment workspace under feedback/targets/, together with the questionnaire that was frozen with it (reader-questionnaire.v1: interest, clarity, voice, emotion and continue, each anchored from one to five and each of which a reader may skip). The identifier of a target is derived from the universe, the version and the scope, so two readers of the same text answer the same frozen copy and no version is frozen twice; the copy is what lets a quoted passage still resolve to the same words after the book has been rewritten, and a response whose target is no longer the accepted version is reported as historical instead of being refused. The frozen copy is captured against the version the reader actually read — a version whose frozen copy is not the accepted text any more is refused as STALE_TARGET rather than frozen again under the reader's answers — and it holds nothing of one reader's session: the report and the findings a reader looked at, their note, what they had already seen of the model's scores or of other readers' comments, and their usefulness and defect reactions are stored on that reader's response, so no later reader inherits a report association they never had.

A response (reader-feedback.v1) is one reader's answers, comments and quotations, anchored to one target and attributed to an identity the server issued for that book (reader-identity.v1), whose kind is team_human for a real submission, model for an annotation and synthetic for a fixture — only the first is a reader saying something, and the counts keep the three apart because the difference between what a reader said and what a model annotated is the point of keeping both. Nothing in reader feedback is averaged, a skipped question stays visible as skipped rather than being read as a low score, and a reader may withdraw a response, which stays readable and marked while its answers are taken back, or correct one of their own with a new response naming the one it replaces. Feedback is written by the server, never by the agent and never inside universes/; the reader interface reaches it through the feedback control in the toolbar of a chapter, its records and limits are fixed in docs/contracts.md §8.7, and the routes that read and write it are in DS005-http-api. The whole evidence can also leave the store as a self-contained snapshot that carries the frozen prose beside the responses, in which readers appear under stable pseudonyms unless the internal snapshot is asked for by name.

Population (feedback)

A population is the group of answers a distribution is computed from: one accepted version of a book together with the scope the readers were shown, the language and the questionnaire version. A chapter reading and a book reading of the same version are two populations, and so are two readings taken with different questionnaires, so their ratings are never added together. Inside a population a reader's latest answer to a question is the one that counts, which is what makes the unit of a distribution the independent reader rather than the submission.

Comparison session

A comparison session is one reader's recorded comparison of two frozen readings of the same book on one question. The store draws the display order once, from the session's identifier, and keeps it, so a refresh shows the two texts in the same places; the answer is one of A, B, tie or unable, with the rationale the reader gave and the conditions they read under. One member of a team is enough to record one, the counts are reported by place and by role apart, and the store claims no effect and no reliability from them.

Stale request

A stale request is a rewrite whose subject changed between the moment the reader asked for it and the moment the book was free to start it. The request is bound to the version it was written for: the hash of the target chapter, the later chapters that existed at that moment, and the pre-chapter state the rewrite must generate from. When the turn reaches the front of its queue the server revalidates that binding, and a target that changed, or a later chapter the request never authorised, is refused with 409 STALE_REQUEST and a message that says what changed. The server never reinterprets an old request against a newer book, because rewriting the wrong version of a chapter is worse than asking the reader again.

Accepted view

The accepted view is what a reader, a review or an edition may rely on: the chapters and the state that the last completed turn left behind. A chapter that a running turn is writing is a candidate, not accepted material, so it is hidden from the universe detail, from a chapter read and from continuation ideas until that turn commits, and a rewrite request is bound against the accepted view rather than against whatever files happen to be on disk. The distinction is what makes a snapshot, an archived version and a printed edition describe the same book rather than a book in motion.

Retry

A retry restarts an interrupted or failed turn with the same request and the same turn number, so the history of a book has no holes and a reader does not have to repeat what they asked for. It is available from the interface and through the retry route, and it is refused with NOT_RETRYABLE for any turn that is still queued, running or already finished, because retrying completed work would duplicate a chapter. A retry of a chapter turn for a closed universe is refused as well; the universe has to be reopened first.

Event stream

The event stream is the server-sent-events route of a universe, used by the reader interface to show work in progress without polling. Without a job parameter it covers every turn of the universe and replays the buffered events of the running one, so a reader who opens the page in the middle of a chapter sees the recent history; with a job parameter it covers that job only. The event types are the job snapshot, a short progress phase, fragments of the agent's live text, tool calls with their result, the end of the turn, an error, and a refresh notice after a turn finished. At most 800 events are buffered per job, and the stream is never the only path to the truth: the universe detail carries the running and waiting jobs, the client falls back to polling the job route when the stream cannot be kept open, and every event is appended to the turn journal as it happens, so the console of a run is read back from the server whether or not anybody was listening.

Continuation ideas

Continuation ideas are concrete next steps derived by the server from the state of a universe, without running the agent: up to two open threads or promises ordered by the chapter in which they are due, at most one deferred answer, the first option of the last chapter's offer, a continuation of the last chapter title when no offer exists, and a suggestion to let time pass. Each idea carries a short label, the prompt it would send and a source that names where it came from. They are what the composer shows when a chapter has no offer, so a reader always has something to press even when the last chapter left no offer behind. The interface does not offer closing a universe, so the ideas never appear in a closing panel.

omp

omp is the Oh My Pi command-line coding agent that writes every chapter and every edition. The server starts one instance per turn, in headless mode, with automatic approval, without a session or a title and with the prompt on standard input, so the agent behaves as a batch writer of files inside the universe folder rather than as an interactive assistant. Its location is taken from the OMP_BIN environment variable with omp as the fallback, and the server probes it at startup and refuses to listen when the probe fails, because a machine that cannot run the agent cannot continue a book. Running the agent as a process is what gives it the file tools that a direct model call would not have, at the cost that the product depends on the binary and on its own authentication.

Model configuration

The model configuration is the single model used for every turn of every universe, taken from the model environment variable with a documented default. It is reported by the configuration and health routes, written into the universe metadata when a universe is created, and written into each turn record, so a book can be read later together with the model that wrote it. There is no per-universe switch, no routing between model tiers and no automatic fallback to a cheaper model: changing the model is an operator decision that affects new turns, and existing records keep the value they were produced with.

Word band

The word band is the pair of minimum and maximum word counts of a chapter, configured by environment and stated in the prompt as the target length. The server measures the written chapter afterwards, stripping headings and splitting the remaining text on whitespace, records a warning when the chapter is below the minimum or far above the maximum, and never fails the turn for length alone. The band exists because a chapter is a reading unit as well as a narrative one: it is reported by the configuration route and used by the server to warn about a chapter outside it. The narrative validator has the same defaults, 900 to 2400 words, and the server passes its configured range on every acceptance run.

Concurrency limit

The concurrency limit is the optional global bound on how many turns run at the same time across the whole server, configured by environment with zero meaning unlimited. It is independent of the queue, which already guarantees that each universe runs at most one turn at a time: the limit protects a machine that hosts many universes at once, while the per-universe rule protects the narrative continuity of a single book. The effective value is reported as a number or as null by the configuration and health routes, which lets an operator confirm what the running process actually applies.

Error code

An error code is the stable machine-readable part of an API failure, delivered inside the error object next to an English message that may be reworded. Codes separate causes that a client must handle differently, such as a law that is too short, a universe that is closed, a rewrite that would drop later chapters, a turn that cannot be retried, an agent that could not run, or a chapter that never appeared on disk. An error can also carry structured fields beside the code, and one of them is the list of chapters that a rewrite would discard. The reader interface reacts to codes rather than to messages, and the failure reference on the operations page lists the codes an operator is likely to meet.

Interfaces

Reader interface

The reader interface is the static browser client in public/: an HTML document, a bootstrap module that binds the DOM and wires the events, further modules under public/ui/ for state, API access, errors, the universe, the slides, the overlays, the actions, the runs with their console, and the event stream, one module per rendered surface under public/ui/render/, a Markdown renderer that builds DOM nodes for chapter prose, and a stylesheet split per surface under public/styles/ — all served by the same server with no build step. It presents a universe as a book of one chapter per slide, shows unfinished work as its own numbered slice in the same reading flow, opens every secondary surface as a dialog over the page, and keeps printing actions out of that flow. It is written in English while the book keeps its own language, and it stores the reading position in the address and in browser storage so that a reload or a shared link returns to the same chapter. It is also the reference consumer of the API: everything it does is a documented route.

Custom tab

The Custom tab is the first tab of the start screen and the only surface that creates a universe on its own: one large field for the specification of a world the reader has in mind — what exists, what is impossible, who is in it and what is already wrong — and one Start control. Pressing it sends the text as the prompt of POST /api/universes with start: true and no template and no ingredient, so the text becomes the fundamental law of the new universe and the first chapter is queued from the same text; a text shorter than a sentence is refused in the panel, and the answer of the server is shown under the same field. It exists because a reader may arrive with a world already in mind, and the two other tabs assume the opposite: the Library offers worlds from the book and the Ingredients compose a world from the table of ideas, and both only put text in the request field for the reader to send.

Composer

The composer is the single card at the bottom of the reader interface that holds ALA's voice and the writing field for the next request. When the current chapter has an offer, the card shows its teaser and up to three compact links whose labels come from that offer, and pressing one fills the field with the prompt it carries without sending anything, so the reader can change the text before sending it. When the chapter has no offer, the first continuation idea is shown instead as a suggestion that fills the field rather than sending it. The field accepts free text, grows with its content up to seven lines (or a little under half of the window height on a short screen) and then scrolls inside itself, can also be dragged taller by its lower edge — a height the reader chose that way is never shrunk back, the automatic growth only raises the lower bound — sends on Enter and inserts a newline on Shift+Enter, and keeps its text when a request cannot be sent, which is why it is also the only input of the start screen before a universe exists.

Dialog

A dialog is how the reader interface presents anything that is not the book itself: a fixed overlay drawn above the page, with a dimmed backdrop, a visible ×, centred on both axes and closed on Esc, on a click on the backdrop and through the ×. A dialog never takes layout space and never pushes the content. There are nine of them: Universes (the button in the header), Edition (the compact ⋯ button, which also carries New universe and the ALA sessions row), What you asked for (the i control of a chapter), Rewrite chapter, ALA sessions (every run of the book with the console of the selected one, opened from the console control of a chapter, from the ⋯ menu, or by the interface itself on the run a writing request, a creation or a review has just started), Review (what to review of one frozen version — this chapter or the whole book — and the reports the book has published), Reports (one published run, opened on the readable report it published), Reader feedback (the questionnaire and what the team said) and Details (a cell, an operator or a family of the table, opened from the Ingredients tab). The Details dialog of a cell shows the idea, the sentence about what that family changes, the work the cell is seen in and the worlds that already use it — the heading counts them — each with a control that opens that template in the Library tab, and it offers one action, Insert in my new world, which becomes In your world and is disabled once the cell is chosen: taking a cell out of the compound is done from the × of its chip in the selection, so a reader never removes an ingredient from a page that describes it. The Library tab works the same way: opening a row only reads a template, and Use on the template is what puts it in charge of the request field. All but Edition keep a tenth of the viewport on every side — 10% above, below, left and right — and hold their content in a body that scrolls on its own, so nothing scrolls just because a line wrapped; Edition is small. Reading is never a dialog, because a chapter always occupies the page, and nothing unfolds in the reading flow: every secondary surface is one of the nine.

Edition metadata

The edition metadata is the file exports/edition.json that the agent writes before the print renderer runs. It carries the title of the edition and, optionally, a subtitle, an author, a year, a dedication, a preface, an afterword and a language; only the title is required, and every missing value is completed from the universe record. The preface and the afterword are Markdown whose first heading becomes their title, the dedication is set on its own page, and the language in this file is consulted before the language of the universe when the renderer resolves the labels of the edition. The renderer reads the file and never writes it, and a file that is not valid JSON fails the command with BAD_JSON rather than being ignored.

Console

The console is the record of what one run did while it worked, read in the ALA sessions dialog and reached from the console icon in the toolbar of a chapter, from the ALA sessions row of the Edition menu and by the interface itself whenever the reader starts something — a writing request, a creation or a review opens the dialog on the run it just started. The dialog lists every run of the open book — the turns ALA wrote a chapter, an edition or an import in, and the separate-phase reviews — under a line that says how many exist right now, and shows the console of the run the reader selects. For a turn that is the turn journal rendered back through GET /api/universes/:id/turns/:number/console: the queued line with the request, the phase lines, every tool call with its outcome, the text the agent displayed and the line that says the run finished or failed, each stamped with its time. For a review it is the run's own console, rendered in the same shape through GET /api/universes/:id/assessments/:runId/console: the facts the record kept — its creation, the evaluator call in flight with its model, its unit and the child's process, every call that finished with its duration and outcome, the continuity substage, and the line that says how it ended — merged in time order with the evaluator journal, the file generated/evaluator.events.jsonl the host appends to while the child works, which holds the text the agent narrates and each tool call with its outcome. A review that needs minutes therefore shows what the agent is reading while it reads it, instead of a bare running line. It is read live while the run works and afterwards for as long as the book exists, and it is the only place in the interface where technical detail about a run is shown — the request that produced a chapter is read in the dialog behind the i control instead.

Turn journal

The turn journal is the file turns/NNNN.events.jsonl beside a turn record: one JSON object per line, each with an at timestamp, appended by the server while the turn runs — the queued line with the request, the running line, every phase line, each tool call with its outcome, the text the agent displayed, and the settle line of a finished or failed run. It is what the console renders, it survives the process that wrote it, and a retry of the same turn appends to the same file instead of replacing it, so the console of a retried turn lists every session of that turn number in order. A journal write never fails a turn, and raw narration text stops being appended once the file passes 400 000 characters while the markers continue.

dropLater

dropLater is the flag a client sends when it accepts that rewriting a chapter removes the chapters that came after it. A rewrite request without the flag is refused with LATER_CHAPTERS when later chapters exist, and the refusal carries the chapter numbers inside the error object so that the interface can state the consequence and ask for explicit confirmation. The flag is the difference between a harmless correction and a destructive edit: with it, the later chapters are archived and removed before the new version of the target chapter is written.

Edition

An edition is the printable form of a book: a DOCX file, a PDF file or both, produced inside the exports/ folder of a universe from its chapters and its editorial metadata. It is requested as an export turn, whose kind on the wire is export, so it takes a place in the same durable queue as a chapter, streams the same progress and can be retried the same way; the turn fails with NO_EXPORT when no file appeared, which is what keeps a reported success equal to a file on disk. The interface treats editions as a discreet feature: the newest file of each format is downloadable from the header menu, and editions are never reading pages. A produced file is served only through the file route of that universe, which accepts edition file names and nothing else.

Edition language

The edition language is the language of the labels of a printed edition: the table of contents heading, the default preface and afterword headings, the chapter label, the fallback title of an untitled chapter and the language metadata inside the file. It is resolved from the command-line option first, then from the language in the editorial metadata file, then from the language of the universe, and finally from Romanian, which lets one book be produced with labels in another language without translating its prose. Romanian and English labels are required; any other code falls back to the English labels.

Book language

The book language is the language of the fiction of a universe, chosen in the header selector before the universe is created. While the book still has no chapters it can be changed through the settings route, and the change rewrites the guidance the server generated — the universe record, the universe's own AGENTS.md and the generated language line of the charter — so the permanent instructions never contradict the book. Once a chapter exists the language is fixed and the change is refused, because the accepted prose is written in that language. It governs everything the agent writes inside the story: chapter titles, prose, dialogue, place names and the offer, and the reader interface marks the rendered prose with it so that a browser applies the correct typography. It is deliberately separate from the language of the interface, which is English, and from the language of the schema, which is English everywhere: file names, plan keys, canon section names, thread kinds and statuses, and atlas identifiers never change with the book.

Skills and tooling

Project skill

A project skill is a folder under skills/ that the repository itself implements and distributes as part of the product: its own instructions, its own reference material, its own scripts and its own dependency record, discovered by the presence of a skill manifest inside the folder. The repository implements seven: the three that write a book — the narrative skill scripta-ala, the print skill scripta-book-export and the import skill scripta-import — and four that are chosen only in a separate design or validation phase, never while a chapter is being written: scripta-story-design, scripta-prose-craft, scripta-continuity-review and scripta-metrics-report. All seven are self-contained so that copying a folder carries everything it needs. A project skill is a product artifact: it is documented here and specified in the design specifications, it is linked into every universe folder, and it is exercised by the project check. Engineering tooling installed for agents is not a product skill and is not part of this documentation.

Skill symlink

A skill symlink is the link .agents/skills/<name> inside a universe folder that points at the canonical skills/<name> in the repository. The server creates one for every project skill when a universe is created and refreshes all of them at startup, replacing a link whose target no longer matches and removing a link whose target is no longer a project skill while leaving real files alone. The links are what make the prompt's references to a skill resolve inside the universe the agent is working in: the agent can read the narrative rules and the file schemas from its own folder, and the print command works from inside the book, without copying skill folders into every universe or installing anything globally.

scripta-ala

scripta-ala is the narrative project skill: the working order, the narrative invariants, the file schemas and the creative palette that the agent must follow when it writes an episode. Its instructions are mandatory rather than advisory, and the prompt of every chapter and rewrite turn tells the agent to read it before writing. It also ships the validator that checks a chapter and the state files around it, which is the only mechanical check of a chapter's shape before the server verifies the result itself. The full contract is documented in its page and specified in DS007-scripta-ala-skill.

scripta-book-export

scripta-book-export is the print project skill: one dependency-free command that turns the chapters and the editorial metadata of a universe into a DOCX file, a PDF file or both. It contains its own font handling, layout engine and document writers, reports its result as a single JSON line with absolute paths, sizes and page counts, and signals failure with documented codes and exit statuses. An export turn asks the agent to write the metadata and run this command, and the produced files are what the reader downloads. The full contract is documented in its page and specified in DS008-scripta-book-export-skill.

scripta-import

scripta-import is the import project skill: the bounded turns that turn an uploaded PDF or DOCX into a universe of its own. It reads the host's extraction, copies the book's own prose into chapters/NNNN-slug.md, derives canon.md, threads.json and atlas.json from that prose, writes the import record, and then runs its own validator over the range the turn wrote. It invents nothing: the prose is the book's and the state files describe it, so an imported book can afterwards be continued and rewritten by scripta-ala, reviewed and measured by the separate-phase skills, and printed like a written one. Its instructions are in SKILL.md and six reference documents, its published contract is schema/import.v1.json, and its command is node skills/scripta-import/scripts/validate-import.mjs --universe <universe-folder> --import <extracted.json> [--chapters <from>-<to>]. The full contract is documented on scripta-import.

scripta-story-design

scripta-story-design is the design project skill: it prepares a book, an arc or a deliberate change of direction before any prose is written, and it ships one validator for the design brief that records those decisions. It is chosen in a separate design phase and is never read during chapter writing, so it adds no step to the chapter prompt. Its boundary is design intent: it does not write a chapter, does not touch the canon, the threads or the atlas, and does not change the permanent law, which belongs to the server and to a human. The full contract is documented in its page and specified in DS010-scripta-story-design-skill.

scripta-prose-craft

scripta-prose-craft is the craft project skill: it holds the decisions a scene needs about focalization, voice, subtext, narrative blocks and rhythm, and it ships one validator for the prose profile that records them. Like the other review and design skills it runs in a separate phase — during design work or a requested revision — and never while a chapter is being written. Its validator applies no aesthetic judgement: it checks structure, identifiers and language codes, and it never counts adjectives, measures sentences or prefers a register. The full contract is documented in its page and specified in DS011-scripta-prose-craft-skill.

scripta-continuity-review

scripta-continuity-review is the continuity project skill: it reads a frozen assessment packet, checks the things a machine can settle — hashes, chapter numbering, references to chapters that do not exist, malformed state containers, a missing canon — and re-verifies any semantic annotations against the same frozen bytes before publishing findings. It is read-only with respect to the book and runs in a separate validation phase, never during chapter writing. The full contract is documented in its page and specified in DS012-scripta-continuity-review-skill.

scripta-metrics-report

scripta-metrics-report is the measurement project skill: it turns a frozen assessment packet plus optional annotations into one validated assessment bundle and the five readable reports rendered from it. Its measurements are advisory, and a measurement whose input is missing is reported as unavailable rather than as a zero. It is a separate-phase skill and never gates a chapter while it is being written. The full contract is documented in its page and specified in DS013-scripta-metrics-report-skill.

Design brief

A design brief is the tentative document scripta-story-design produces and validates, stored as proposal/story-design.json in an assessment workspace under the schema version design.v1. It records the central idea, the premise, the open thematic question, the reader promise, the character and relationship directions, the world assumptions and the flexible arcs of a book or an arc. It is tentative by construction: a planned ending can be replaced without rewriting history, and an event it plans becomes part of the book only when accepted prose establishes it. A sparse brief with empty optional fields is legal, because a recorded gap is more useful than an invented certainty.

World assumption

A world assumption is a statement about a book's world together with its epistemic kind, which is one of law, observation, belief, social_rule, hypothesis or plan. A law is the permanent constraint of the universe; an observation is something the prose showed happening; a belief belongs to a character and may be false; a social rule is an institution the characters maintain; a hypothesis stays unresolved; and a plan is a future intention that is never evidence that an event occurred. The distinction is what stops a character's theory from being read as the physics of the world and stops a planned event from entering the canon.

Prose profile

A prose profile is the document scripta-prose-craft produces and validates, stored as proposal/prose-profile.json in an assessment workspace under the schema version profile.v1. It records the intended reader experience, the narrator, the focalization and its limits, a few character voice notes, the purposeful recurring devices and the revision priorities of a book or a scene. It exists so that a review can name a defect in a passage and propose a bounded change instead of asking for more adjectives, and its validator checks structure only: no adjective quota, sentence-length rule or preferred register is ever part of it.

Evidence reference

An evidence reference identifies the exact text a finding or a judgement came from: the relative path of a file inside an assessment packet, that file's SHA-256, a zero-based half-open range of UTF-8 byte offsets and the quoted text at that range. A review re-computes the hash and the quoted slice before publishing a finding, so a quotation that does not match the frozen source, or one that has gone stale because the book changed, is reported as an integrity problem instead of being treated as proof.

Continuity finding

A continuity finding is one reported defect or observation about a book's continuity, produced by scripta-continuity-review as part of a continuity-result.v1 result. It carries a stable identifier, a kind — an integrity problem in the input itself, a contradiction, an unsupported change, a reference to a chapter that does not exist or an editorial observation — a severity, a certainty, a status of confirmed, unresolved or dismissed, and the evidence it rests on. Findings are advisory: a suspected semantic error does not by itself block publication, and the author of the book decides what to revise.

Advisory score

An advisory score is a measurement produced by scripta-metrics-report that informs a human decision without deciding it. Each measurement carries a status: computed when deterministic code measured it, judged when it rests on an annotated judgement, not_assessable when its input does not exist, not_applicable when the question does not apply and error when a method failed. An unavailable measurement is never rendered as a zero, a missing input never silently redistributes the weight of an aggregate, and no score can start a rewrite by itself.

Assessment workspace

An assessment workspace is where the separate design and review phases keep their files, outside every universe folder: <workspace>/<universe-id>/<accepted-version>/<run-id>/ with input/ for the frozen packet, proposal/ for a design or craft proposal, approval.json for the human decision on that proposal, result/ for the published result of a review and run.json for the record of the run. The workspace never holds accepted narrative state and is never written by a writing turn: it is the only place a review or a design may write, and a later writing request carries an approved direction from it as input rather than sending ALA to read a skill. The layout is a convention of the host; the skills themselves take explicit --input and --out paths, so any authorized equivalent directory works (contracts §8).

Accepted version

An accepted version is the narrative content of a universe at one moment, identified by a hash of that content rather than by the folder, a timestamp or the universe identifier: every file whose role is chapter, offer, canon, threads or atlas contributes its path, hash and byte count to the identity, while universe.json and assessment inputs such as a design, a profile or a corpus do not, because the server rewrites the record on every turn and the others are not narrative content. Two versions of the same book therefore share an identity exactly when their chapters and state files are identical, which is what lets a review declare the version it judged and a rewrite mark an older result historical instead of silently rebinding it (contracts §8.2).

Assessment packet

An assessment packet is a frozen copy of one accepted version of a book, used as the input of a review. It is a directory whose manifest.json declares the universe, the version identity, the capture time, the book, the declared scope and one entry per file with a relative path, a SHA-256 hash, a byte count, a role and an artifact identifier; the files themselves sit at those relative paths beside the manifest. A consumer rejects a packet whose manifest is unreadable, whose schema version it does not know, whose paths are duplicated, absolute or escaping, whose files are missing or whose hashes, sizes or recomputed version identity do not match. The declared scope is part of the contract: complete requires every chapter and the state containers, partial names what it contains and declares what it omits, and textual_only carries prose alone, so a review of language cannot be mistaken for a clean book.

Validator

The validator is the read-only script of the narrative skill that reads a universe folder and reports what is wrong with a chapter before the agent commits to its answer. It checks the chapter file and its title, the Markdown subset, the presence of the plan and of every plan key, the offer, the thread list, the atlas identifiers and node states, and the canon sections, and it separates hard errors from warnings such as a chapter outside the word band or an overdue thread. It writes nothing, prints one JSON line and each finding, and exits with a nonzero status when it found an error. Malformed state is answered with a structured report instead of a stack trace. The agent must fix every error and run it again, the server runs the same validator as the structural gate of a chapter turn, and the project check runs it against a complete fixture to prove that it returns ok: true with the current file contract.

Check script

The check script is scripts/check.mjs, run by npm run check, and it verifies the whole technology chain of the project without spending model budget. It checks the Node.js version, probes the agent binary, asserts the skill manifests exist, creates temporary universes, exercises the law and language validation, asserts that skill links are installed, writes a chapter and reads it back through the store, closes and reopens a universe, runs the validator and the print renderer and parses their JSON reports, lists universes, and reproduces a restart in which a queued turn stays on disk, a running turn becomes interrupted, the canon is rolled back and the partial chapter disappears. It also asserts the periodic table data, since the table is a data contract: one hundred and eighty unique cells whose systematic symbol is the letter of the family followed by the letter of the operator, a cell opened with the idea, the sentence about what its family changes and the nights that already use it, the resolution of ingredients and the composition of the law, the rule that neither the law nor the ingredient lines nor the AGENTS.md of a universe writes a two-letter symbol where a sentence belongs, the mapping of the creation request body in which premise wins over opening and a template fills only the gaps, the library against its folders, a night template with cells and a central problem and the universe composed from it, the store lock, and the fact that importing the server module starts no server and takes no lock. It exits with a nonzero status if any check failed and always removes the universes it created, which makes it safe to run on a working instance.
